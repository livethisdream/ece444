    Array Factor and Beamwidth

    The aperture measured in wavelengths, \(Nd/\lambda\), sets the beamwidth, and steering widens it by \(1/\cos\theta_0\).

    Lesson 20 · Antennas, Phased Arrays, and Radar Systems · Dr. Neil Rogers

    Printing

    To print the slides, switch to present mode and print: one slide a sheet, with room for notes. Print from read mode for the whole lesson.

    Learning Objectives

    1. I can derive the array factor for an arbitrary linear array and apply pattern multiplication.

    The Uniform Array Factor

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} AF_N(\psi) &= \frac{\sin(N\psi/2)}{N\ \sin(\psi/2)} \\ \psi &= kd(\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0) \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    • The L18 Lab beam measured about 13° at boresight and wider at 45°; this function produces both.

    • The peak sits at \(\psi\) = 0, which is \(\theta = \theta_0\).

    • The midterm project measured the same widths, nulls, and sidelobes from a pattern cut.

    The Antenna Pattern Measurement midterm project, assigned in Lesson 9, measured all of this from the other direction. The quantities you extracted from a measured cut — the half-power width, the null positions, the sidelobe level referenced to the peak — are exactly the quantities this lesson computes from theory, so keep your project data at hand.

    In the L18 Lab you steered the array across the room and watched the beam widen as it went. The main lobe was about 13° across at boresight and noticeably wider at 45°, and the pattern sat on a floor of sidelobes that moved with the beam. Lesson 16 gave us the closed form that produces all of that. Today we extract numbers from it: the half-power beamwidth of a steered array, the angle of every null, the directivity, and the design step in reverse, which takes a beamwidth specification and finds the array size.

    Lesson 16 summed \(N\) equal-amplitude elements with a progressive phase and collapsed the geometric series into the normalized uniform array factor

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} AF_N(\psi) &= \frac{\sin(N\psi/2)}{N\ \sin(\psi/2)}, \\ \psi &= kd(\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0). \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    Everything about the shape of the pattern is in that one function of \(\psi\). The array’s physical parameters enter only through the map from angle to \(\psi\), and the beam peak is wherever \(\psi\) = 0, which is \(\theta = \theta_0\).

    The Sinc Near the Peak

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} AF_N &\approx \frac{\sin(N\psi/2)}{N\psi/2} \\ &= \frac{\sin u}{u}, \qquad u \equiv \frac{N\psi}{2} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    • Near the peak \(\psi\) is small, so \(\sin(\psi/2) \approx \psi/2\).

    • The array is a sampled aperture of length \(L = Nd\), and this is L6’s uniform line-source pattern.

    Near the peak, \(\psi\) is small, so \(\sin(\psi/2) \approx \psi/2\) and

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} AF_N &\approx \frac{\sin(N\psi/2)}{N\psi/2} = \frac{\sin u}{u}, \\ u &\equiv \frac{N\psi}{2}. \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    That is the same \(\sin u / u\) we found in Lesson 6 for a uniform line source, and the reason is physical: an \(N\)-element uniform array with spacing \(d\) is a sampled aperture of length \(L = Nd\). Near the peak, where the sampling is too fine to matter, the array’s pattern matches the continuous aperture’s. The length is \(Nd\) rather than the \((N-1)d\) between the end elements because each element accounts for one spacing \(d\) of aperture, the sampled line source of Lesson 16.

    Half-Power Beamwidth

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \frac{N\psi}{2} &= 1.392 \\ \sin\theta - \sin\theta_0 &= \frac{0.443\ \lambda}{Nd} \\ \theta_\text{HP} &\approx \frac{0.886\ \lambda}{Nd\ \cos\theta_0} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    • \(\sin\theta\) has slope \(\cos\theta_0\) at the beam, so the width in angle grows by \(1/\cos\theta_0\).

    Power is half the peak where \(\lvert \sin u/u\rvert = 1/\sqrt{2}\), which happens at \(u\) = 1.392. Solving outward from there:

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \frac{N\psi}{2} &= 1.392 \\ kd(\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0) &= \frac{2.784}{N} \\ \sin\theta - \sin\theta_0 &= \frac{0.443\ \lambda}{Nd}. \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    The last step fixes the half-power width in \(\sin\theta\), and that width is the same at every scan angle, because the array factor depends only on \(\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0\). The scan angle enters when we convert it back to an angle. The derivative of \(\sin\theta\) is \(\cos\theta\), so near the beam peak the first term of its Taylor series gives

    \[\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0 \approx (\theta - \theta_0)\cos\theta_0.\]

    At broadside \(\sin\theta\) is steepest, with slope 1, and a step in \(\sin\theta\) is the same step in angle. Toward the horizon \(\sin\theta\) flattens, and the same step in \(\sin\theta\) covers more angle: at 60° the slope is 0.5, so the beam is twice as wide. The figure shows the course array’s half-power band in \(\sin\theta\) mapped back to angle at both scan angles. For a beam that is narrow compared with its distance from broadside, the half-width is

    \[\theta - \theta_0 \approx \frac{0.443\ \lambda}{Nd\ \cos\theta_0}\]

    and the full width is twice that:

    \[\theta_\text{HP} \approx \frac{0.886\ \lambda}{Nd\ \cos\theta_0}.\]

    The aperture measured in wavelengths, \(Nd/\lambda\), sets the beamwidth. The constant 0.886 is the uniform line-source constant from Lesson 6 and Lesson 15, unchanged, because a uniform array of length \(Nd\) is that line source sampled at \(N\) points. Steering off broadside foreshortens the aperture as seen from the beam direction, and the beam widens by \(1/\cos\theta_0\).

    The same factor has a physical reading, shown in Lesson 18’s figure: seen from the beam direction, the array’s projected length is \(Nd\cos\theta_0\), and a shorter aperture gives a wider beam.

    Scan Broadening of the Course Array

    • \(N\) = 8, \(d\) = 14 mm, worked at 10.3 GHz: \(Nd\) = 3.85 \(\lambda\).

    • Broadside 13.2°; steered to 30°, 15.2°.

    • At 60° the rule gives 26.4° and the pattern 30.5°: a wide-scanned beam is always wider than the rule.

    Worked example — the PHASER array

    The course array has \(N\) = 8 patches on \(d\) = 14 mm centers. We work this example at 10.3 GHz; your kit’s Find HB100 frequency will differ, and the L20 Lab has you recompute at it. At 10.3 GHz, \(\lambda\) = 29.1 mm, so \(d/\lambda\) = 0.481 and the aperture is \(Nd\) = 112 mm = 3.85 \(\lambda\).

    Broadside: \(\theta_\text{HP}\) = 0.886/3.85 = 0.230 rad = 13.2°.

    Steered to 30°: 13.2°/cos 30° = 15.2°, against 15.4° from the pattern.

    Steered to 45°: 13.2°/cos 45° = 18.7°.

    Steered to 60°: the formula gives 26.4°, while the pattern itself is 30.5° wide. The small-angle step above is the approximation that fails, and it underestimates the width — a wide-scanned beam is always wider than \(0.886\lambda/(Nd\cos\theta_0)\) predicts.

    Null Locations

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \frac{N\psi}{2} &= m\pi, \quad m = 1, 2, \ldots \\ \sin\theta &= \sin\theta_0 \pm \frac{m\lambda}{Nd} \\ \text{FNBW} &= 2\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{Nd}\right) \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    • Equal 0.260 steps in \(\sin\theta\) become 15.1°, 16.3°, 19.9° in angle; the fourth null has no real angle.

    A null is a zero of the numerator that is not also a zero of the denominator. The numerator vanishes when

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \frac{N\psi}{2} &= m\pi, \qquad m = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \\ \psi &= \frac{2\pi m}{N} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    except when \(m\) is a multiple of \(N\), because then \(\sin(\psi/2)\) vanishes as well and the ratio returns to unity. Those are the main lobe and its repeats, the grating lobes of Lesson 23, not nulls. Converting through \(\psi = kd(\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0)\):

    \[\sin\theta = \sin\theta_0 \pm \frac{m\lambda}{Nd}.\]

    The nulls are equally spaced in \(\sin\theta\), not in \(\theta\). The figure plots the same broadside pattern twice. The top panel uses \(\sin\theta\) as its axis, and there the nulls are a comb with teeth every \(\lambda/Nd\) = 0.260. The bottom panel uses angle, and the dashed lines carry each null down to it: the gaps grow from 15.1° to 16.3° to 19.9°, because \(\sin\theta\) flattens toward the horizon, the same slope argument that widened the steered beam. A null exists only if the right-hand side lands inside the visible region \(\lvert \sin\theta\rvert\) ≤ 1; values outside it correspond to no real direction, and there is no null.

    Taking \(m\) = 1 on both sides of a broadside beam gives the first-null beamwidth

    \[\text{FNBW} = 2\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{Nd}\right).\]

    Anatomy of a Uniform-Array Pattern

    • \(\lambda/Nd\) = 0.260: nulls at 15.1°, 31.3°, and 51.2°; the fourth falls outside the visible region.

    • FNBW is 30.1°, about 2.3 times the half-power width.

    • The nulls are sharp, so a coarse sweep can miss them.

    Worked example — nulls of the course array

    With \(Nd/\lambda\) = 3.85, the tooth spacing is \(\lambda/Nd\) = 0.260 in \(\sin\theta\). Broadside, the nulls sit at

    \(\sin\theta\) = 0.260 → 15.1°, 0.520 → 31.3°, 0.780 → 51.2°, \(\quad 1.040 \rightarrow\) outside the visible region.

    So the eight-element array shows three nulls per side, not seven, and FNBW = 2(15.1°) = 30.1°. Steer the same array to 30° and the first nulls land at \(\sin\theta\) = 0.5 ± 0.260, which is 13.9° and 49.5°. The beam is no longer symmetric about its own peak: the lower null sits 16.1° below it and the upper null 19.5° above, for a first-null beamwidth of 35.6°.

    The null-to-null width runs about 2.3 times the half-power width for a uniform array. That ratio is worth remembering when you read a measured sweep, because the nulls are sharp features that a coarse angle grid can miss entirely, while the half-power points are easy to interpolate.

    Sidelobe Level

    Beamwidth and null positions scale with \(Nd/\lambda\); the sidelobe level does not. A uniform array holds its first sidelobe near −13 dB at 8 elements or 800. L22’s taper lowers it and widens the beam.

    Note

    Beamwidth and null positions both scale with \(Nd/\lambda\); the sidelobe level does not. A uniform array holds its first sidelobe at about −13 dB whether it has 8 elements or 800. Lesson 22 changes that number by changing the amplitude distribution, and it widens the beam to do it.

    Directivity of a Uniform Array

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} D &= \frac{4\pi\ U_\text{max}}{P_\text{rad}} \\ &= \frac{\text{peak of } \lvert AF\rvert^2}{\text{average of } \lvert AF\rvert^2} \\ &\approx \frac{2Nd}{\lambda} = \frac{2L}{\lambda} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    • Peak: the \(N\) fields add in phase, so \(\lvert AF\rvert^2\) = \(N^2\).

    • Average over the sphere: \(N\) at \(d = \lambda/2\), so \(D = N\).

    • Doubling \(N\) adds 3 dB by halving the beamwidth.

    Lesson 2 defined directivity as the peak radiation intensity over its average over the sphere, \(D = 4\pi U_\text{max}/P_\text{rad}\). For an array of isotropic elements the intensity follows \(\lvert AF\rvert^2\), so we need its peak and its average. Write the unnormalized array factor as a sum over elements, with \(\gamma\) the angle from the array axis (so \(\cos\gamma = \sin\theta\) in the plane of the array), and multiply it by its conjugate:

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} AF &= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} e^{jknd\cos\gamma} \\ \lvert AF\rvert^2 &= \sum_{m}\sum_{n} e^{jkd(m-n)\cos\gamma}. \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    At the peak every term is 1, so the peak of \(\lvert AF\rvert^2\) is \(N^2\): the \(N\) fields add in phase. The average over the sphere goes term by term. A term with \(m = n\) is 1 everywhere. A cross term averages to a sinc of the separation \(x = d(m-n)\):

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \left\langle e^{jkx\cos\gamma} \right\rangle &= \frac{1}{4\pi}\oint e^{jkx\cos\gamma}\ d\Omega \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\int_0^\pi e^{jkx\cos\gamma}\sin\gamma\ d\gamma \\ &= \frac{\sin kx}{kx}. \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    The ratio is the directivity:

    \[D = \frac{N^2}{\displaystyle\sum_{m}\sum_{n} \frac{\sin\!\left(kd(m-n)\right)}{kd(m-n)}}.\]

    At \(d = \lambda/2\) every cross term contains \(\sin(\pi(m-n))\), which is zero, and the double sum collapses to the \(N\) diagonal terms:

    \[D = \frac{N^2}{N} = N = \frac{2Nd}{\lambda} \quad \text{at } d = \lambda/2.\]

    Written in that last form, the result carries over to other spacings: for any uniform broadside array with \(d\) below a wavelength,

    \[D \approx \frac{2Nd}{\lambda} = \frac{2L}{\lambda},\]

    which is the long uniform line-source directivity from Lesson 6 with \(L = Nd\) — the same sampled-aperture argument that produced the 0.886. Directivity grows linearly with aperture in wavelengths. Doubling the element count at fixed spacing doubles \(D\) and adds 3 dB, and it does so by halving the beamwidth, not by making any element radiate harder.

    Directivity of the Course Array

    \[D \approx 2(8)(0.481) = 7.7 = 8.9\ \text{dB}\]
    \[G_\text{total} = G_\text{element} + D_\text{array}\]

    • The center four alone give 5.9 dB, 3 dB less, and twice the beamwidth.

    • Steering widens the beam but holds \(D\) near 8.9 dB; scan loss comes from the element (L21).

    Worked example — directivity of the course array

    \(D\) ≈ 2(8)(0.481) = 7.7, or \(10\log_{10}(7.7)\) = 8.9 dB. Turning off four elements to leave the center four gives \(D\) ≈ 3.85 = 5.9 dB, exactly 3 dB less, and doubles the beamwidth.

    That 3 dB is the drop in directivity, and the L20 Lab measures a different quantity, the received peak. On receive, the array adds the element voltages in phase, so the peak voltage is proportional to \(N\) and the peak power to \(N^2\). Halving the active elements therefore drops the ideal received peak by 6 dB: 3 dB because half the elements collect half the power, and 3 dB because the beam’s directivity halves. The dry-run kit read 5.1 and 4.8 dB per halving, so expect about \(5 \pm 1\) dB on your own kit. The cause of the shortfall is still open, and it does not touch the beamwidths, which the lab grades.

    The figure is the L20 Lab’s, drawn at the dry-run kit’s 10.408 GHz.

    The eight elements are patches, not isotropic radiators, so the array’s gain is the element gain plus the array gain. Pattern multiplication becomes addition in dB:

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} G_\text{total}\ [\text{dBi}] &= G_\text{element}\ [\text{dBi}] \\ &\quad + D_\text{array}\ [\text{dB}]. \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    Eight half-wave dipoles, at 2.15 dBi each, would make an 11.0 dBi array. The sum holds while the element pattern is close to flat across the main lobe, and Lesson 21 takes up the case where it is not.

    Directivity under steering. The array-factor directivity of a linear array barely changes as you steer, even though the beam visibly widens. The main lobe of a linear array is a cone about the array axis; steering off broadside thickens the cone wall by \(1/\cos\theta_0\) and shrinks its circumference by \(\cos\theta_0\), leaving the solid angle nearly unchanged. For the course array the array-factor directivity is 8.86 dB at broadside, 8.91 dB at 45°, and 9.12 dB at 60°. The scan loss you measure on real hardware comes from the element pattern, not from the array factor, which is why the PHASER loses gain toward the edges of its scan volume. That is Lesson 21’s subject.

    We can run every result so far in reverse. If we fix the beamwidth and scan volume as requirements, we can design the array using the following three-step process.

    1. Aperture. Invert the half-power formula, with \(\theta_\text{HP}\) in radians:

      \[Nd = \frac{0.886\ \lambda}{\theta_\text{HP}}.\]

      If the specification has to hold across a scan volume, divide by \(\cos\theta_0\) at the widest scan angle, because that is where the beam is widest.

    2. Spacing. Lesson 23 derives the condition that keeps grating lobes out of visible space:

      \[d < \frac{\lambda}{1 + \lvert\sin\theta_0\rvert_\text{max}}.\]

      At \(d = \lambda/2\) the criterion holds for any scan angle, which is why half-wavelength spacing is the default.

    3. Element count. \(N = Nd/d\), rounded up. Every element is an antenna, a phase shifter, an amplifier and a control line, so this is the number that sets the cost of the array.

    Three Details in the Prediction Table

    • The 0.886 rule assumes a narrow beam; check that before using it at \(N = 2\).

    • Check whether each first null lands at a real angle.

    • Samples sit one 2.8125° phase step apart: about 0.9° near broadside.

    The 0.886 rule carries a small-angle step, and the wider the beam, the worse that step gets. Before you use it for a small aperture, decide whether the beam is narrow enough; where it is not, solve the array factor’s half-power point directly. For two elements the array factor is just \(\cos(\psi/2)\), which you can solve by hand.

    The null formula \(\sin\theta = m\lambda/Nd\) only gives a null where the right-hand side is at most 1. Check that for each aperture before you write a first-null beamwidth down, and decide what that means for the sidelobes too.

    The first sidelobe drifts with element count. The −13 dB figure is the large-array limit; eight elements give −12.8 dB, and smaller arrays land higher. The sweep’s noise floor sits roughly 23 dB below the uniform-taper peak.

    The sweep is a discrete grid. With Use Bits on, as in every lab preset, the GUI steps the element-to-element phase one phase-shifter LSB, 2.8125°, per point. One step moves \(\sin\theta\) by 2.8125°/173.2° = 0.0162, which is 0.93° of angle at broadside and 1.07° at 30°, widening as \(1/\cos\theta\). A half-power width read as the span between the outermost samples above −3 dB comes out short by up to one step: 12.1° to 13.1° for the full array, against 13.3° from the pattern. Interpolating between the two samples that straddle each −3 dB crossing recovers the width to within 0.1°. The nulls are read at their lowest sample, which puts the measured FNBW between 30.1° and 31.1°. That spread comes from the sampling grid, not from the array.

    Lesson 21 addresses what the array factor leaves out. Every result in this lesson treats the elements as isotropic points, which is why the directivity came out independent of scan angle and why the pattern in the widget is the same at 60° as at boresight. Real patches have their own pattern, it rolls off toward the edges of the scan volume, and the full array pattern is the product of the two. That product is where the array’s measured gain, its usable scan range, and its cross-polarization all come from.

    The L20 Lab puts your predictions in front of the hardware. You will load the Array Factor preset, sweep the full array, then drop to four elements and two, then leave only the two end elements on, and compare measured beamwidths against the numbers you calculated in Part 1 of the packet. Bring Part 1 filled in; a measurement you cannot predict within a couple of degrees is a measurement you cannot use to find a fault later. Read the L20 Lab procedure before you go to the bench.
