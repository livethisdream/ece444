    Array Factor

    Switch elements off, one sweep at a time, and measure what the beam does.

    L20 Lab · Antennas, Phased Arrays, and Radar Systems · Dr. Neil Rogers

    Learning Objectives

    1. I can derive the array factor for an arbitrary linear array and apply pattern multiplication.

    E1 and E8

    Lesson 20 showed that the beamwidth of a uniform array depends on the array only through its active aperture, \(Nd\), measured in wavelengths. Today you test that on your kit. You sweep the beam past a fixed HB100 three times, switching off elements at the ends of the row so that \(Nd\) halves between sweeps, and read the half-power beamwidth, the first-null beamwidth, the first sidelobe, and the peak off each trace. A fourth sweep leaves only E1 and E8 on: two elements again, like Step 9, but spread across the whole row. Each sweep fills one row of Table A in Part 2 of the packet, and the E1 and E8 sweep also fills Table B. You check every reading against the prediction you made in Part 1.

    Predict First

    Before you sweep, predict each trace, then check yourself. Switch the elements to match a row of the table above and decide, before you press Show pattern: how wide the main lobe is next to the dashed eight-element pattern, how far the peak drops, and how many full-height lobes there are. Then show it. Any change to the elements hides the pattern again, so each guess comes first. The widget draws the ideal array factor at 10.0 GHz, not at your kit’s frequency, so its numbers are close to your Part 1 values but not equal to them; the kit’s trace differs from both in the ways Lesson 20’s ”What the Sweep Shows” lists.

    Bring Up the Kit

    • HB100 at boresight, 1 m out, at patch-row height; it stays there for all four sweeps.

    • Find HB100, Calibrate, then the Array Factor preset.

    • Set Rx Gain for a −10 dBFS peak.

    Place the HB100 at boresight, about 1 m from the face of the array, at the same height as the patch row and with its face square to the array. Boresight placement matters more than distance in this lab: every prediction is a broadside number, and a source offset by a few degrees shifts the whole trace along the angle axis. Leave it there for the whole lab, because you compare the four traces against each other at the end.

    1. Connect. Browse to your kit’s address from Lesson 17 and wait for the pill to read Connected.

    2. Find the source. With the HB100 at boresight, press Find HB100 and record the Signal Freq in Part 2 of the packet. If it is more than 50 MHz from the frequency you used in Part 1, recompute your calculated columns before you sweep; a smaller change moves no prediction by more than about 0.5°.

    3. Calibrate the array. Press Calibrate and wait for it to finish, with the HB100 still at boresight. An uncalibrated array has amplitude and phase errors spread across the row, which fills the nulls and raises the sidelobes on its own, and you would then be measuring the calibration rather than the array factor. If the broadside sweep in Step 6 does not look right, fix that first.

    4. Load the preset. Open Lab Presets and press Array Factor; its buttons respond only while the section is open. The preset sets a uniform taper on all eight elements, zero steering phase, and Use Bits on, so each sweep point is one 2.8125° phase step. It opens the Rectangular tab.

    5. Set the gain. Set Rx Gain (dB) for an FFT peak of \(-10 \pm 3\) dBFS, and record it. The preset does not set it, and the right value depends on your source, its battery, and its distance; about 1 dB is a reasonable start with the source 1 m out. A flat-topped main lobe on the Rectangular tab means the receiver has clipped, so turn the gain down. Leave it there for all four sweeps, so the peaks compare.

    6. Check the broadside sweep. Press Start. On the Rectangular tab, the sweep should peak near 0°, with its first sidelobes near ±22° and 11 to 14 dB below the peak. If they are higher than that, or differ from each other by more than a few dB, run Calibrate again before you go on.

    Sweep Four Apertures

    • For each row: set the Element Gains, Start, then Freeze.

    • Record Peak Gain, HPBW, FNBW, and first SLL in Table A.

    • Read levels in dBc, from each trace’s own peak.

    • Ignore the column standing at ±90°.

    Before each sweep, check your Part 1 row for it. For each one, press Start, then Freeze to hold the trace, and record four numbers in the boxes under the step, which fill Table A: the Peak Gain readout; the HPBW, between the two angles where the trace falls 3 dB below its peak; the FNBW, between the two minima that bracket the main lobe; and the first SLL, the tallest sidelobe.

    Read every level in dBc: subtract the trace’s own peak from the reading, so the peak is 0 dBc. Peak Gain reads in dBFS and drops each time you switch elements off, so only a level taken from its own trace’s peak compares with the array factor and with the other traces. The last few dozen points of every sweep ask for more phase than any real angle needs, and all of them plot at ±90°, so the trace stands up in that column. That column is the array factor at the extra phase, not a lobe: leave it out of Steps 7 to 10.

    1. Eight elements. With all eight Element Gains at 100, sweep. Your HPBW should land within a degree of your calculated value.

    2. Four elements, E3 to E6. Set E1, E2, E7, and E8 to 0, and sweep. The peak drops by the change you predicted in Part 1, or up to about 1.5 dB less on the kit. A null that fills to the noise floor reads wide, so this FNBW can come out a few degrees wider than your prediction.

    3. Two elements, E4 and E5. Set every element to 0 except E4 and E5, and sweep. The peak drops from the eight-element peak by your predicted change, or up to about 3 dB less on the kit. A trace that keeps falling toward ±80° is the two-element array factor’s own roll-off toward endfire, not a fault.

    4. Two elements, E1 and E8. Set E4 and E5 to 0 and E1 and E8 to 100, and sweep. Record in Table A the peak, the change from eight elements, and the HPBW and FNBW of the lobe at 0°, and in its First SLL box the level of the first full-height lobe on either side of it, \(m = \pm 1\). Record in Table B the angle and level of every full-height lobe, numbered \(m = -3\) to +3 from the most negative angle, \(m = 0\) at 0°. The \(m = \pm 4\) lobes need more phase than any real angle and stand at full height in the ±90° column; leave them out.

    Compare and Restore

    • Overlay the three frozen uniform traces.

    • Does the beamwidth scale as \(1/Nd\)? Does each peak step match your Part 1 voltage sum?

    • Restore all eight Element Gains to 100.

    1. Compare the frozen traces. The three uniform apertures are on the plot together. Set each HPBW and FNBW beside its \(Nd\), and each peak change beside the voltage sum you used in Part 1, from Lesson 20’s ”Halving the Active Aperture”. The kit reads up to about 1.5 dB less than the voltage sum per halving, 3 dB less over both; a shortfall that size is within the spread of its readings. Part 3 asks you to explain what you see.

    2. Restore the array. Set all eight Element Gains back to 100, so the next lab starts from a full uniform aperture.

    No Hardware?

    Note

    Run python phaser_headless.py --sim and open the same URL. The lab reproduces step for step in simulation, because the simulated source already sits at boresight and nothing in the procedure requires moving it. Its Signal Freq is 10.525 GHz, so compute the calculated columns there. It adds the element signals exactly in voltage, so its peak steps come out at the ideal voltage-sum value, without the kit’s shortfall of up to 1.5 dB, and it does not model Rx Gain (dB). Record those as your measured column and reconcile them against the calculated column exactly as you would with the kit.

    Lab Packet

    The L20 Lab packet is one submission for the whole lab, turned in complete before Lesson 21. It has three parts:

    1. Part 1, Pre-Lab (individual, before the lab period, because you need it in class): your kit’s frequency, the prediction table for the three uniform apertures, the array factor and lobe angles for E1 and E8, and a rehearsal of the E1 and E8 sweep on the hosted simulator.

    2. Part 2, Measurements (your own kit’s data, recorded in class): Table A, one row per sweep, and Table B, the E1 and E8 lobes.

    3. Part 3, Writeup (individual, after the lab): reconcile the measurements with the predictions, and two analysis problems.

    • L20 Lab packet (PDF)

    For Part 3

    Part 3 of the packet is the writeup, done after class with your Part 2 entries beside you. Its first question asks three things:

    1. Does the beam double at each halving? Expect the eight- and four-element HPBW within about a degree of your Part 1 values, and the two-element HPBW within several degrees. Name the cause of any larger difference.

    2. Why do the E1 and E8 peak and the E4 and E5 peak agree while their beams do not? Use where each pair puts its weight along the row.

    3. How do your two peak changes compare with the voltage sum’s step, given the spread of your readings?

    Lesson 20’s ”What the Sweep Shows” gives the expected size of each difference. The two analysis problems that follow use Lesson 20’s results, and the last part of the second compares a Hann taper, from Lesson 22, with switching elements off.
