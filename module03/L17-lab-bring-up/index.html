    L17 Lab — Phased Array Bring-Up#

    ECE 444 · Fall 2026

    Phased Array Bring-Up

    Bring your own kit up from a blank card, then prove it works.

    L17 Lab · Antennas, Phased Arrays, and Radar Systems · Dr. Neil Rogers

    Learning Objectives

    1. I can identify the hardware architecture of the ADALM-PHASER and control it via SDR software.

    This lab is the bench half of Lesson 17. The lesson walked the receive chain block by block; here you bring up that hardware yourself, from a blank card to a beam sweep that follows the source. The lab packet records both: that the kit came up, and that it works.

    Part A: Build Your PHASER

    • Each cadet builds their own kit from a stock Linux image: flash, connect, set the clock, join the Wi-Fi, provision, calibrate.

    • The Ethernet cable carries ssh and the browser; the Wi-Fi only connects the Pi to the internet.

    Each cadet starts with their own PHASER kit and a microSD card and builds the kit up from the operating system: you flash ADI’s stock Kuiper Linux image onto the card, connect to the Pi over a cable, set its clock, put it on the Wi-Fi, run the Phaser provisioning script, and calibrate the array. These are the steps that bring up any embedded Linux device, and each one fails in a way that teaches you something about the device. Part B then makes the first measurements on the kit you built.

    The laptop and the kit use two connections for two different jobs. A direct Ethernet cable from the laptop to the Pi carries ssh and the browser interface. The classroom Wi-Fi is only the Pi’s route to the internet, which it needs to download the Phaser software.

    The cable is there because of how guest networks are run. A guest network can isolate its clients from one another, so a laptop and a Pi that have both joined the classroom Wi-Fi may be unable to reach each other even though each one reaches the internet. A cable between the two does not depend on the network’s settings, so every step that talks to the Pi from the laptop uses the cable.

    Each cadet has their own kit:

    • the ADALM-PHASER board, with the Raspberry Pi and the ADALM-Pluto attached on the back

    • an HB100 microwave source on its own small stand or battery holder

    • one USB-C supply, for the PHASER board, which powers the Pi too

    • a camera tripod, for the board and for aiming the source

    • a 32 GB microSD card and an Ethernet cable

    • a laptop with balenaEtcher and an ssh client

    Your instructor assigns your kit a two-digit number, NN. Kit NN is named phaser-NN, and kit 03 appears in every example from here on; use your own number.

    On Windows the ssh client is the OpenSSH Client, under Settings > Apps > Optional features; macOS and Linux include one.

    The instructor also keeps fallback cards, copies of one finished kit. A fallback card gets a kit running when a build cannot be finished in the period; it is named for your kit before it goes in, and it still needs Steps 19 to 22, because the calibration it carries belongs to the instructor’s kit.

    Before Class: Download

    1. Install balenaEtcher.

    2. Download Kuiper 2021_R2, ADI’s Linux image for the Pi.

    Use exactly this release. Later Kuiper releases break the provisioning script.

    Do Steps 1 to 5 at home before the period: they need only your laptop and the card. The file is image_2023-04-02-ADI-Kuiper-full.zip, and Etcher reads the .zip as it is, with no unzipping.

    Kuiper 2021_R2 is the release the Phaser software is tested on, and it is based on Debian 11, bullseye. The later Kuiper releases move to a newer Debian that changes the parts of the system the provisioning script and the Wi-Fi steps rely on: where the boot files live and which program manages the network.

    Before Class: Format and Flash

    1. Format the card FAT32: right-click it, Format…, Start.

    2. Etcher: Flash from file, the .zip, the card, Flash!

    3. Cancel any Windows offer to format it afterward.

    Format first, or Etcher can fail 10 minutes in.

    The write and its verification take about 15 to 20 minutes. Leave the card in until Etcher says the flash is complete.

    Etcher writes the whole card, so whatever was on it is gone; check that the target it shows is the microSD card and not a laptop drive before you press Flash! After the write, the card holds two partitions: a small FAT one that holds the boot files, which Windows can read, and a Linux one, which it cannot. That is why Windows offers to format part of the card, and formatting it would erase the image.

    The card’s 32 GB is the reason for the FAT32 in Step 3: Windows offers FAT32 only on cards of 32 GB or less.

    Assemble and Power On

    1. Mount the PHASER on the tripod.

    2. Card in the Pi; Pluto USB to the Pi; Ethernet to the laptop.

    3. USB-C supply into the PHASER, by the SMA cable.

    Power only through the PHASER’s USB-C, never the Pi’s.

    Mount the board with its patch face vertical and its row of patches horizontal: the array steers in the plane of that row, so a board mounted on its side steers in elevation, while every later lab sweeps the beam in azimuth.

    The PHASER board supplies the Pi through its own connector, and powering the Pi directly bypasses the board’s supply. Unplugging the kit while Linux is running can also corrupt the card; see the last frame of Part A for the safe way to power down.

    First Contact

    1. Wait 1 to 2 minutes, then:

    ssh analog@analog.local

    Answer yes; the password is analog.

    No router, no setup: the laptop and the Pi find each other over the bare cable.

    analog is the name, the user, and the password that ADI’s Kuiper image ships with. The yes answers ssh’s question about whether to trust the kit’s host key, which it has never seen. If ssh says it cannot resolve analog.local, wait another minute and try again.

    Nothing on a bare cable hands out addresses. After about a minute of asking, the Pi and the laptop each give themselves a link-local address, one in 169.254.x.x that only the cable can carry, and the laptop finds the Pi by name with multicast DNS, the .local lookup. That is why a direct cable works with no setup on either end, and why the first minute after power-on shows nothing. On the instructor’s kit, hostname -I showed exactly one address at this point, 169.254.210.227.

    Set the Clock

    1. Seed the clock from a web server, then check it:

    sudo date -s "$(curl -sI http://deb.debian.org/ | sed -n 's/^[Dd]ate: *//p')"
date

    sudo asks for the password, analog. The date that comes back should be today’s date and time; the server reports it in GMT, and the Pi shows it in its own time zone.

    A Raspberry Pi has no battery-backed clock, and Kuiper ships with no network time client, so a fresh card boots believing it is the day the image was made, in April 2023. Every secure download that follows checks the server’s certificate against today’s date, and a certificate is only valid between two dates, so a wrong clock makes the download fail with a certificate error before any software arrives. The provisioning script installs a time client and fixes the clock for good, but it has to be downloaded first.

    The command breaks that circle with a plain, unencrypted request, which no certificate guards: curl -sI fetches only the headers of a Debian web page, sed keeps the line that begins Date:, and date -s sets the clock to it.

    Join the Wi-Fi

    1. Set the Wi-Fi country and add the classroom network:

    sudo raspi-config nonint do_wifi_country US
sudo tee -a /etc/wpa_supplicant/wpa_supplicant.conf > /dev/null <<'EOF'

network={
    ssid="AF_ACADEMY_GUEST"
    key_mgmt=NONE
}
EOF

    1. Run sudo reboot, wait a minute, and ssh in again as in Step 9.

    2. Run ping -c 4 github.com and expect four replies.

    The classroom Wi-Fi is the open guest network AF_ACADEMY_GUEST. The reboot in Step 12 is not optional: on the instructor’s kit, asking the Wi-Fi client to reread its settings without a reboot left it offline, and the ping failed until the Pi restarted. If the ping fails after the reboot, tell the instructor.

    Kuiper 2021_R2 is based on Raspberry Pi OS bullseye, where wpa_supplicant joins the Wi-Fi network and dhcpcd then requests an address on it. The Wi-Fi country is a regulatory setting: the channels a radio may use depend on where it is, so the operating system keeps the Wi-Fi radio blocked (rfkill) until a country is set. The network block names the network, and key_mgmt=NONE tells wpa_supplicant it has no password. tee -a appends the block with root privileges, which a plain >> redirection would not have.

    Two things must hold on an open guest network for this step and the next, and the instructor checks both before class. The network must not put a captive portal, a sign-in or terms page, in front of a new client, because the Pi has no browser to click through one. It must also allow outbound HTTPS, because the provisioning script downloads the Phaser software from GitHub.

    Provision the Kit

    1. Run the provisioning script, with your kit’s name:

    curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/livethisdream/phaser/main/scripts/provision.sh | bash -s -- --hostname phaser-03

    1. Wait for Provisioned., then run sudo reboot.

    2. Run ssh analog@phaser-03.local, answer yes, and check that hostname prints phaser-03.

    The script prints its steps, [1/8] through [8/8], and takes tens of minutes; step [7/8], which builds the Python library that talks to the board, is the long one and can sit quiet for minutes. Leave it running.

    The -s -- in Step 14 matters. Without the -s, bash takes --hostname for the name of a script file and stops with bash: --hostname: No such file or directory.

    provision.sh turns a stock Kuiper card into a PHASER kit. In order, it checks the machine, installs a time client and fixes the clock for good, installs the system packages, adds the PHASER board’s device-tree overlay to the boot configuration, sets the hostname, installs the plumbing that lets the Pi talk to the Pluto over USB, builds pyadi-iio, ADI’s Python library for the board, and finally runs install.sh, which installs the Phaser backend and the browser interface as a service that starts at every boot.

    The reboot in Step 15 is what makes the board appear. The device-tree overlay is read only at boot, and it is the file that tells Linux which chips sit on the board’s bus; until the Pi restarts with it, the operating system does not know the phase shifters exist. The new hostname also takes effect at boot.

    Every kit needs its own name because every kit also joins the same Wi-Fi. Twenty kits all calling themselves phaser.local collide on multicast DNS, and the losers rename themselves phaser-2, phaser-3, and so on, so a name you type can reach someone else’s kit or none at all.

    The yes in Step 16 appears again because the laptop has never seen a kit named phaser-03 before. Running the installer again later is how a kit is updated: curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/livethisdream/phaser/main/install.sh | bash.

    Check the Service

    1. Run journalctl -u phaser-headless -n 30 and look for the banner ADI Phaser Beamforming Backend with no No device found after it.

    2. Browse to http://phaser-03.local:8080 and wait for the pill to read Connected.

    The backend runs as a service named phaser-headless, and its log is the first place to look when anything is wrong. A healthy start prints the banner, then lines such as Initializing SDR... and Connecting to SDR at ip:192.168.2.1..., and keeps going.

    Exception: No device found after Connecting to SDR means the Pi cannot see the Pluto. Check its USB cable to the Pi; the service retries every few seconds and comes up on its own once the Pluto appears.

    The log is a better check than systemctl is-active phaser-headless. A service that fails at start-up is restarted by systemd every five seconds, and between failures it reads active; on the instructor’s kit it had restarted 83 times while the status still said active. The backend also starts the hardware before the web server, so a missing Pluto shows up in the browser only as a page that refuses to connect. 192.168.2.1 is the Pluto’s own address on the USB link between it and the Pi.

    The Phaser GUI

    Today you need five sidebar controls, Find HB100, Characterize IF, Calibrate Phaser, Signal Freq, and Rx Gain, and the FFT tab.

    The pill sits at the bottom right and changes from Checking… to Connected. Start stays disabled until the backend is ready.

    If the pill never reads Connected, turn on Show Logs Tab under Plot Options and read the Logs tab: a message such as “Start blocked until backend is ready” says the backend is still starting, and the page will connect on its own once it is up.

    The interface has a sidebar of control sections on the left and a plot area with tabs on the right. You will use all of these over the next several lessons; today only a few matter.

    The plot tabs are Rectangular, Polar, FFT, and Tracking, plus Logs when Show Logs Tab is on. Start runs the beam sweep, and nothing plots until you press it.     The FFT tab refreshes once per sweep. Freeze stores up to three reference traces on the Rectangular and Polar tabs, and pressing and holding it clears them; it is unavailable on the FFT tab.

    Find, Characterize, Calibrate

    1. Place the powered HB100 \(1\ \text{m}\) out at boresight.

    2. Press Find HB100 and record its frequency.

    3. Press Characterize IF and record the IF it chooses.

    4. Press Calibrate Phaser.

    In order. Let each one finish before the next.

    Set the HB100 about \(1\ \text{m}\) in front of the array, at the same height as the patch row, with its own patch face toward the board. Each button opens a Calibration window that reports progress; wait for it, and do not press another button while it runs. Leave Reboot alone.

    One meter is the shortest far-field distance for this array. Lesson 5’s far-field boundary, with the aperture \(D = Nd = 8 \times 14\ \text{mm} = 112\ \text{mm}\) and \(\lambda = 28.5\ \text{mm}\), is

    \[\frac{2D^2}{\lambda} = \frac{2\ (0.112\ \text{m})^2}{0.0285\ \text{m}} = 0.88\ \text{m},\]

    so moving the source closer than about \(0.9\ \text{m}\) raises the signal but curves the arriving wavefront enough to distort the patterns of later labs.

    Find HB100 sweeps the band for the source and records its frequency; expect a value within a few hundred MHz of \(10.525\ \text{GHz}\), since every HB100 sits somewhere different. Before it saves anything it checks the answer: it retunes \(4\ \text{MHz}\) to each side and confirms the tone stays at the same frequency, which a real source does and a signal made inside the receiver does not.

    Characterize IF finds the intermediate frequency this kit receives cleanly at, the frequency the board converts the \(10\ \text{GHz}\) signal down to before the Pluto digitizes it. It sweeps the IF against the source it just found and picks the middle of the range that works.

    Calibrate Phaser measures the per-element gain and phase offsets of the array itself, the small differences between the eight channels caused by trace lengths and part tolerances, and stores them so that zero commanded phase across the array produces a broadside beam. Leave the source at boresight while it runs. It first checks that the array is hearing the source at all, by switching every element off and on; with no difference between the two, it stops and says so rather than calibrating against nothing.

    Each step needs the one before it. Characterizing the IF needs a tone at a known frequency, and calibrating needs the kit tuned to the source at an IF it can hear. On the instructor’s kit, calibration first ran at the IF that ships in the configuration file, \(2.2\ \text{GHz}\), and heard nothing: on/off contrast \(0.93\ \text{dB}\). That kit receives cleanly only from \(1.2\) to \(2.175\ \text{GHz}\), while a second kit works at \(2.2\ \text{GHz}\); the usable IF differs by about \(200\ \text{MHz}\) between two kits built alike, which is why every kit measures its own. At the IF it measured, \(1.69\ \text{GHz}\), the same calibration ran to completion with \(32.7\ \text{dB}\) of contrast. On a kit that has never been characterized, Calibrate Phaser runs the characterization first, or stops and says so.

    The frequency matters downstream because the steering phase depends on it. Lesson 18 derives the progressive phase

    \[\Delta\phi = kd\sin\theta_0, \qquad k = \frac{2\pi f}{c},\]

    so the same steering angle needs a different phase at a different frequency. For a \(30^\circ\) steer the ramp is \(84.9^\circ\) per element at \(10.1\ \text{GHz}\) and \(89.9^\circ\) at \(10.7\ \text{GHz}\), and a ramp computed for the wrong frequency points the beam at the wrong angle.

    All three results are stored in calibration.json on the Pi and survive a reboot, so a kit is calibrated once and stays calibrated until it is moved to a different source.

    No hardware?

    Part A has no simulated version, because every step acts on the card, the Pi, or the network. If your kit will not come up, tell the instructor. The hosted simulator covers Part B.

    Power Down and Troubleshooting

    1. To power down, press the button on the back of the PHASER, or hold the Connected pill for two seconds; wait until the lights go out, then unplug the USB-C.

    One or two red power lights may stay on after shutdown; those are fine. Pulling the plug while Linux is running can corrupt the card, and a corrupted card means flashing it again. A software reboot, sudo reboot, shuts down cleanly and is always safe.

    Part B: First Measurements

    1. Press 1 Steering Angle under Lab Presets.

    2. Set Rx Gain (dB) to 10 and press Start.

    3. Record the FFT peak’s position, its level, and the floor, in dBFS.

    Expect at least 20 dB between peak and floor.

    Part B runs on the kit you brought up in Part A and records the observations any working kit produces, for Part 2 of the lab packet. Work through the steps in order.

    The preset loads the workshop’s initial state, with a uniform taper, Phase Control at zero, and the beam commanded to broadside, and opens the FFT tab; Start begins the sweep. The presets do not set Rx Gain, whose start-up value comes from the Pi’s configuration file, so step 2 sets the 10 dB that every later lab uses as its reference. dBFS is decibels relative to the ADC’s full-scale input.

    In the FFT tab, a single narrow peak should stand well above a flat noise floor, near \(0\ \text{MHz}\), and its separation from the floor, in dB, should be unambiguous: at least 20 dB with the source at \(1\ \text{m}\), the smallest separation seen on the bench at that distance. Less than that points to a misaimed or distant source, or a weak battery in the HB100.

    Lesson 2’s Friis equation sets the peak,

    \[P_r = P_t G_t G_r \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi R}\right)^2;\]

    doubling the distance lowers it by \(20\log_{10}2 = 6.0\ \text{dB}\), and misaiming the HB100 lowers its \(G_t\). On the receive side, Lesson 20’s broadside directivity of the uniform array is

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} D &\approx \frac{2Nd}{\lambda} \\ &= \frac{2 \times 8 \times 14\ \text{mm}}{28.5\ \text{mm}} \\ &= 7.86 \quad (8.96\ \text{dB}) \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    at \(10.525\ \text{GHz}\) (7.7 at the workshop’s \(10.3\ \text{GHz}\)), the \(G_r\) of the Friis equation.

    The floor in the FFT is the noise in one frequency bin, so the peak-to-floor separation also depends on the FFT length: a longer FFT narrows each bin, lowers the floor, and raises the separation with no change at the antenna.

    The Sweep at Boresight

    1. Open the Rectangular tab; turn on Show Peak Angle Marker under Plot Options.

    2. Record Est. Angle and Peak Array Gain with the source at boresight.

    Expect Est. Angle within \(2^\circ\) of \(0^\circ\).

    The Rectangular tab plots the beam sweep: the backend steers the beam across the field of view one phase step at a time and records the received power at each step, so the trace peaks where the beam points at the source.

    The Sweep Follows the Source

    1. Carry the HB100 to about \(30^\circ\) on one side, still \(1\ \text{m}\) out and aimed at the array. Record both again, and which end, E1 or E8, it moved toward.

    2. Return it to boresight and record both once more.

    The source sits at \(30^\circ\) when it is \(0.5\ \text{m}\) to one side of the array’s centerline and still \(1\ \text{m}\) from the array. The peak should move with it, to about \(30^\circ\), and drop by a dB or so, because each patch receives less from the side. Returned to boresight, the trace comes back to where it started. The sign of the moved reading records which side the GUI calls positive, which the L18 Lab uses.

    On a working kit the sweep’s floor sits about 23 dB below the uniform-taper peak. A normal FFT peak with a sweep that stands well short of that, or that peaks far from \(0^\circ\) with the source at boresight, points to the calibration rather than the signal: check that Phase Control reads zero, then put the source back at boresight and run Calibrate Phaser again. Lesson 18 derives why the sweep peaks at the source, and the L18 Lab measures it on a protractor arc.

    No hardware?

    Open livethisdream.github.io/phaser, the same dashboard with no install. At a station whose hardware has failed, add ?sim=1 to the station’s URL or turn on Simulator Mode under Configuration. An orange SIMULATION pill marks simulated data.

    Steps 1 to 5 of Part B behave as described, except that the tone appears at \(-1\ \text{MHz}\) and the simulator does not model Rx Gain. Steps 6 and 7 cannot be done, because the simulated source is fixed at boresight. Find HB100 and Calibrate Phaser run scripted scans.

    With a checkout of the Phaser repository and Python, python phaser_headless.py --sim serves the same page at http://localhost:8080.

    Lab Packet

    The L17 Lab packet is one submission for the whole lab, turned in complete before Lesson 18. It has three parts:

    1. Part 1, Pre-Lab (individual, before the lab period): the receive chain in order, the phase shifter’s step, the element and channel counts, and how a kit number sets the kit’s name.

    2. Part 2, Measurements (your own kit’s data, recorded in class): the bring-up record from Part A, and the kit check from Part B, the FFT peak and the sweep with the source at boresight, moved, and back.

    3. Part 3, Writeup (individual, after the lab): a labeled block diagram, two bench diagnoses, and two written answers.
