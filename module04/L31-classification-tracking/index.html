    Classification and Tracking

    Read four targets’ micro-Doppler on your kit, then classify recorded targets and track a walker in range and angle.

    Lesson 31 · Antennas, Phased Arrays, and Radar Systems · Dr. Neil Rogers

    Printing

    Learning Objectives

    1. I can integrate FMCW radar processing with a phased-array front-end to track a moving target.
    2. I can classify a detected target from its micro-Doppler signature and report the classifier's accuracy.
    Module 4 25 26 27 28 29 30 31 Report in class final project

    By the end of the L30 Lab the radar produces a list of detections, each a range, a radial velocity, and an SNR. A detection says that something is there and how fast it is closing. It does not say what the target is, and one detection per frame does not say where the target is going. This page adds the last two stages of the chain your final project report explains. The lesson comes first, and you read it before the lab: it classifies a detected target from its micro-Doppler signature, which Lesson 26 introduced, measures how often the classifier is right, and then joins the detections into a track in range, with an alpha-beta filter, and in angle, with the monopulse comparison of the L24 Lab. Its last frame, the Pre-Lab, lists the predictions you make before the lab. The lab follows it on this page.

    Micro-Doppler of a Moving Part

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} f_D(t) &= \frac{2}{\lambda}\left[v_\text{body} + v_\text{part}(t)\right] \\ v_\text{part}(t) &= \Omega\rho\ \sin(\Omega t) \quad \text{(a rotor)} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    • A rotor’s tips set the edge of its band, \(\pm v_\text{tip}\).

    • A walker’s torso sets a line off zero, and its limbs swing about it once a stride.

    Lesson 26 derived the Doppler shift of a rigid target, \(f_D = 2v_r/\lambda\). A real target is rarely rigid. Its body moves at one radial speed, and its parts move about the body at speeds of their own, so each part’s echo carries the body’s Doppler plus a shift from its own motion. The extra shifts are the micro-Doppler, and they change in time with the motion that causes them.

    A point on a rotor at radius \(\rho\), turning at \(\Omega\) radians per second and seen edge-on, swings toward and away from the radar along the line of sight, so its radial speed is \(\Omega\rho\sin(\Omega t)\) and its Doppler swings sinusoidally between \(\pm 2\Omega\rho/\lambda\). Every point along a blade does this at its own radius, so a blade fills the band from zero out to its tip. The tips move fastest. For a 25 cm fan at 700 rpm,

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} v_\text{tip} &= \pi D\ \frac{\text{rpm}}{60} \\ &= \pi(0.25)\frac{700}{60} = 9.2\ \text{m/s}, \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    which is \(\pm 2(9.2)/0.030 = \pm 611\ \text{Hz}\) at 10.0 GHz. A rotor held at a tilt \(\psi\) off edge-on moves only \(\cos\psi\) of that along the line of sight, so the same fan turned 40° shows tips at \(9.2\cos 40^\circ = 7.0\ \text{m/s}\).

    A walker’s arms and legs swing forward and back about the torso, once a stride, like pendulums. A walker closing at 1.1 m/s whose limbs swing 2.3 m/s about the torso puts the torso at 73 Hz, and the limbs reach \(1.1 + 2.3 = 3.4\ \text{m/s}\), or 227 Hz, on the forward stroke, and \(1.1 - 2.3 = -1.2\ \text{m/s}\) on the back stroke. The body’s Doppler says how fast the target is moving. The micro-Doppler says what is moving, which is the property a classifier needs.

    Four Signatures

    • The walker shows a torso line off zero, and its limbs swing once a stride.

    • The fan shows a steady band out to its tips.

    • The spinner’s band piles up in horns near its tips.

    • The hand shows one trace swinging through zero.

    The L31 Lab reads four kinds of target: you walking, a desk fan, a fidget spinner, and your hand waving. The figure simulates the lesson’s example of each on the example burst, one range bin sampled once per chirp, with the spectrogram the next frames build. Each panel has its own span, and the right-hand axis reads the same scale in m/s, the unit of the app’s view.

    The walker is the only target that goes anywhere, so it is the only one whose pattern sits off zero: the torso is the steady line at 73 Hz and the limbs trace arcs above and below it, once a stride. The fan stays put, so its band is centered on zero, and it is steady, because the blades pass too often for any one of them to appear. The spinner, 6 cm across at 1500 rpm, is a smaller rotor, with tips at \(\pi(0.06)(25) = 4.7\ \text{m/s}\), or 314 Hz. Its weight sits in three lobes near the rim rather than along a blade, and a point moving sinusoidally spends most of its time near its extremes, so its band piles up in two horns near the tip speed instead of filling evenly. The hand is one scatterer moving back and forth. A wave 0.15 m each way at 1.2 Hz peaks at \(2\pi(1.2)(0.15) = 1.1\ \text{m/s}\), or 75 Hz, and the trace crosses zero twice a cycle, so the hand has no average Doppler at all.

    In the app’s Range-Doppler view the same targets appear at their ranges, across velocity: watched for a few seconds, each one’s band spans the speeds its parts reach, and the walker’s sits off zero.

    One Range Bin in Slow Time

    • One sample per chirp: 2.5 kHz on the example burst, so ±1.25 kHz, or ±18.75 m/s.

    • A part faster than that aliases.

    • A walker crosses a 0.375 m bin in 0.34 s: sum the bins around it.

    Lesson 29 forms the range-Doppler map with an FFT across the chirps of a burst, one FFT for each range bin. That FFT runs in slow time: one sample per chirp, from the same range bin of each chirp’s range profile. A spectrogram is the same FFT taken over short, overlapping pieces of a longer recording instead of over one whole burst, so each column of the spectrogram is a Doppler spectrum of a few tens of milliseconds of the target.

    The slow-time samples arrive at the PRI, 0.4 ms on the example burst, so the sample rate is 2.5 kHz and the spectrogram covers ±1.25 kHz, the ±18.75 m/s that L29 calls \(v_\text{max}\). A part faster than that aliases, as in L26, and wraps to the wrong Doppler. A 30 cm fan with tips at 18.8 m/s sits just past that edge and its tips wrap. A CW radar samples its Doppler tone directly, hundreds of kHz instead of once a PRI, so the same tips do not wrap there.

    Two more details make the spectrogram show the target and nothing else. Stationary returns, the walls and the fan’s own stand, sit at 0 Hz and are far stronger than the parts that move; subtracting the mean of the slow-time samples removes them, which is what MTI does at 0 Hz. And a target that moves in range leaves its bin: at 1.1 m/s the example walker crosses a 0.375 m bin in 0.34 s, so the spectrogram of one bin shows the walker in pieces. The provided spectrogram therefore sums the few bins around the detection’s range, which the L30 detection list supplies. You run it and tune its window; you do not write it.

    Window Length

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \Delta f &= \frac{1}{T_w} \\ \delta f &= \dot f_D\ T_w = \frac{2a}{\lambda}\ T_w \\ T_w &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\dot f_D}} \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{867\ \text{Hz/s}}} = 34\ \text{ms} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    • The example walker’s limbs accelerate at \(13.0\ \text{m/s}^2\), so 64 chirps, 25.6 ms, is the nearest power of two.

    • A fan’s tips change too fast for any window to follow, so its band looks steady.

    Every spectrogram trades resolution in frequency against resolution in time. A window of length \(T_w\) resolves Doppler shifts \(\Delta f = 1/T_w\) apart, the \(\Delta f = 1/T\) of L26, so a long window resolves finely. But a limb’s Doppler does not hold still during the window. If it changes at a rate \(\dot f_D\), it moves by \(\delta f = \dot f_D T_w\) while the window is open, and the column smears that far. A Doppler rate comes from an acceleration \(a\) along the line of sight, \(\dot f_D = 2a/\lambda\), by differentiating \(f_D = 2v/\lambda\).

    The resolution improves as the window grows and the smear worsens, so the best window makes the two equal, \(1/T_w = \dot f_D T_w\), which gives \(T_w = 1/\sqrt{\dot f_D}\). The example walker swings its limbs 2.3 m/s about the torso, 0.9 times a second, so their peak acceleration is \(2\pi(0.9)(2.3) = 13.0\ \text{m/s}^2\) and

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \dot f_D &= \frac{2(13.0)}{0.030} = 867\ \text{Hz/s}, \\ T_w &= 34\ \text{ms}, \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    about 85 chirps of the example burst. Windows come in powers of two:

    A measured line is wider than \(1/T_w\). The Hann window that spectrogram applies widens a line’s main lobe to 1.44 bins at its 3 dB points, the widening Lesson 28 gives for a line in range, and a part that moves during the window widens it further, so expect a line about \(1.44/T_w\) wide or more.     A fan’s tips change their Doppler far faster than any window can follow, so a fan’s band shows steady, averaged over many blade passes, and a very short window shows each blade’s flash instead.

    Three Features

    • The centroid is the body’s radial speed.

    • The spread is how fast its parts move about it.

    • The periodicity is how strongly the pattern repeats at the pace of a stride or a wave.

    A classifier does not look at a picture; it compares numbers. A feature is one number computed from the spectrogram that takes different values for different classes of target, and three are enough to separate most of the lab’s four kinds of target. Each measures a different physical property, which is why they separate targets that any one of them alone would confuse.

    The provided features returns all three, in m/s for the first two, after ignoring each column’s bins more than 15 dB below its peak so the noise floor does not count as motion. The centroid is the power-weighted mean Doppler, converted to speed: the body’s radial speed, near zero for a target that stays put. The spread is the power-weighted width of each column about that mean: how fast the parts move relative to the body. The periodicity asks whether the pattern repeats every 0.25 to 2 s, as a stride or a wave does: a walker or a waving hand scores near 0.8, and a steady rotor scores low, about 0.2.

    The features are a list of numbers, one row per record, and the classifier compares the rows. Adding a feature is one more number on the end of that list: the lab’s task (b) appends one with a single np.append line in the notebook’s my_features, and the classifier uses it with no other change.

    Nearest-Centroid Classifier

    • Training stores each class’s mean.

    • A trial takes the nearest mean’s class.

    A nearest-centroid classifier is the simplest one that learns from data. We record a set of training trials of each target, compute the three features of each, and keep each class’s mean, the crosses in the figure. A new trial gets the class whose mean is nearest. The features have different units, so the classifier first scales each one to a common size; without that, the feature with the largest numbers would decide every distance.

    The figure plots 20 simulated training trials of each example target on the example burst: walkers at speeds from 0.7 to 1.5 m/s and up to 60° off the line of sight, 25 cm fans from 450 to 850 rpm, 6 cm spinners from 900 to 2000 rpm, waves of different sizes and rates, and an SNR of 3 to 15 dB a chirp. The walkers are the only class well off zero in centroid, with a mean of 0.78 m/s. Most hands have the least spread among the others, under 1 m/s, though a few slow, large waves reach into the rotors’ range, and the right panel shows what sets the hands apart: periodicity. The fans and spinners share zero centroid and low periodicity, so only their spread separates them, a mean of 4.7 against 3.6 m/s, and the two clouds overlap: a fast spinner looks like a slow fan.

    • Accuracy on unseen trials is \(64/80 = 80\%\).

    • Fourteen of the 16 errors mistake one rotor for the other.

    The confusion matrix counts, for each true class (a row), how many trials the classifier called each class (a column). The diagonal holds the correct calls, and every count off the diagonal is a specific mistake. Its accuracy is the diagonal’s total over all trials,

    \[\text{accuracy} = \frac{20 + 16 + 8 + 20}{80} = 80\%.\]

    The trials in the matrix are a second set of 20 per class, drawn the same way as the training set but not used to train. A classifier scored on its own training trials has already seen the answers, and its accuracy overstates what it will do on a target it has not seen. The lab scores each record with a classifier trained on all the others, leave-one-out, which keeps that rule when there are only a few records of each class.

    The matrix says more than the accuracy does. Fourteen of the 16 errors are a fan called a spinner or a spinner called a fan, the overlap the previous figure showed, and the other two are slow fans whose small spread falls nearer the hands’ mean. Walkers and hands are never wrong. An 80% classifier that never misses a person is a different instrument from one that misses people 20% of the time, and only the matrix tells them apart. It also says what to improve: a feature that separates the two rotors would address most of the errors. Finally, the number is an estimate. Five different draws of 80 test trials score between 80% and 84%, so with 20 trials a class report the counts, not only the percentage.

    The Band’s Edge and Shape

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} v_\text{rms} &= v_\text{tip}\sqrt{\left\langle \sin^2 \Omega t \right\rangle \left\langle (\rho/R)^2 \right\rangle} \\ &= v_\text{tip}\sqrt{\tfrac12 \left\langle (\rho/R)^2 \right\rangle} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    • The edge is the tip speed; a tilt or a rate scales it.

    • The shape, spread over edge, says where the weight sits.

    Two more features can separate the rotors. The edge is the speed at the outside of each column’s band, the bins within 15 dB of the column’s peak, averaged over the record: for a rotor it is the tip speed along the line of sight, \(v_\text{tip}\cos\psi\). The spread measures the same motion in a different way, as an RMS. Both grow with the rate and both shrink with the tilt, so a fast spinner and a slow or tilted fan can share either one.

    Their ratio does not. Every point on a rotor moves at \(v_\text{tip}(\rho/R)\sin\Omega t\) along the line of sight, so its RMS speed over a turn and over the points is the first line above. The mean of \(\sin^2\) over a turn is \(\tfrac12\). The mean of \((\rho/R)^2\) depends only on where along the radius the weight sits. For a blade whose weight is spread evenly over its outer third, from \(\rho = 2R/3\) to \(R\),

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \left\langle (\rho/R)^2 \right\rangle &= \frac{1}{R/3}\int_{2R/3}^{R}\frac{\rho^2}{R^2}\ d\rho = \frac{1 - (2/3)^3}{3(1/3)} = 0.704, \\ \frac{v_\text{rms}}{v_\text{tip}} &= \sqrt{\tfrac12(0.704)} = 0.59. \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    A tilt or a rate scales every speed in the band by the same factor, so the shape, the spread over the edge, is the same at any tilt and any rate: it measures where the weight sits, not how fast it moves. A rotor whose weight sits farther out has a larger shape.

    The figure normalizes each held spectrum to its own edge. The tilted blade lands on the untilted one, because a tilt scales every speed by the same \(\cos\psi\); the rim and the outer third differ from the full blade because their weight sits elsewhere. The notebook’s task (b) adds the edge and then the shape to the three features and compares the matrices.

    The Alpha-Beta Filter

    • Each frame: predict where the target should be, compare with the detection, correct.

    • \(\alpha\) sets how much a new detection moves the track.

    • On a missed frame, the track only predicts.

    A detection list is noisy. Each range is the center of a range bin, a walker’s body is larger than a bin, some frames miss the target, and nothing in the list says that the detection at 3.1 m in one frame is the same person as the one at 3.4 m in the next. A track joins the detections of one target into a single estimate of its range and velocity, frame after frame. A military radar calls a person on foot a dismount; tracking one is the second half of the L31 Lab.

    The alpha-beta filter is the simplest tracker, and the provided alpha_beta runs it. Each frame it predicts where the target should be, assuming it kept its velocity over one frame, compares that prediction with the frame’s detection, and corrects the range and the velocity by fixed fractions of the difference, the residual. The gain \(\alpha\) sets how far a new detection moves the range, and \(\beta\) how far it moves the velocity. A frame with no detection near the prediction keeps the prediction, which is how a track coasts through a miss, and accepting only the detection nearest the prediction is how two targets keep separate tracks. On the example burst one frame is \(\Delta t = 102.4\ \text{ms}\).

    Choosing Alpha and Beta

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \beta &= \frac{\alpha^2}{2-\alpha} \\ \frac{\sigma_\text{track}}{\sigma_z} &= \sqrt{\frac{2\alpha^2 + 2\beta - 3\alpha\beta}{\alpha(4 - 2\alpha - \beta)}} \\ \text{lag} &= \frac{a\ \Delta t^2}{\beta} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    The two gains set how much weight the track gives a new detection. A large \(\alpha\) follows each detection closely, noise and all; a small one averages over many frames and smooths the noise, but it is slow to notice a change in velocity, so it lags a target that turns. The usual choice ties the two gains together, \(\beta = \alpha^2/(2-\alpha)\), the Benedict-Bordner choice, which balances the track’s noise against its lag and leaves one number to choose.

    The other two lines say what that number trades. For detection noise \(\sigma_z\) that is independent from frame to frame, the track’s settled RMS error is \(\sigma_\text{track}\), the second line. Under a steady acceleration \(a\) the track trails the target by the third line. The target gains \(a\Delta t\) of speed each frame, and once the track settles its velocity must gain the same, so the velocity correction \((\beta/\Delta t)e\) equals \(a\Delta t\) and the residual is \(e = a\Delta t^2/\beta\). Work both for \(\alpha = 0.4\) on the example burst:

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \beta &= \frac{0.4^2}{2 - 0.4} = 0.100, \\ \frac{\sigma_\text{track}}{\sigma_z} &= \sqrt{\frac{2(0.16) + 2(0.1) - 3(0.4)(0.1)}{0.4\left(4 - 0.8 - 0.1\right)}} = 0.57. \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    A walker who turns by swinging from +1.2 to −1.2 m/s over 1 s, as a cosine, has a peak acceleration of \(\pi(1.2) = 3.8\ \text{m/s}^2\), so the settled lag would be

    \[\text{lag} = \frac{3.8\ (0.1024)^2}{0.100} = 0.40\ \text{m}.\]

    A small \(\alpha\) gives a low noise ratio and a long lag, and a large one the reverse; the choice depends on how much the target maneuvers.

    The figure simulates that walk on the example burst: out from 2 m at 1.2 m/s, the one-second turn, and back. The detections are the centers of the 0.375 m bins the walker falls in, with 0.12 m of body jitter, 0.15 m RMS from the truth in all, and 5 of the 79 frames missed. With \(\alpha = 0.4\) the track holds 0.12 m RMS on the straights, better than the detections, and is off by at most 0.23 m at the turn, less than the settled 0.40, because the turn ends before the lag builds up. With \(\alpha = 0.15\) the track is smoother, but it trails the turn by up to 0.9 m and is still catching up on the way back. The track’s velocity is a second check: it should agree with the Doppler velocity of the detections it accepts.

    Angle by Monopulse

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \Delta\phi &= \frac{2\pi D}{\lambda}\ \sin\theta \\ \theta &= \arcsin\frac{\lambda\ \Delta\phi}{2\pi D} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    • Two subarray channels \(D = 56\ \text{mm}\) apart give the angle in one look, with no sweep.

    • Unambiguous within ±15.5° of broadside at 10.0 GHz.

    • Read in the detection’s range-Doppler cell.

    Range comes from the FMCW processing; angle comes from the array. Lesson 19 found a bearing by sweeping the beam across the field and taking the peak, one sweep at a time. The L24 Lab measured it in one look with monopulse, comparing the two subarray channels, and that is the method a tracker needs.

    The two subarrays’ centers sit four elements, \(D = 56\ \text{mm}\), apart. A wave from angle \(\theta\) reaches one center a path \(D\sin\theta\) before the other, so the two channels differ in phase by

    \[\Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi D}{\lambda}\sin\theta,\]

    672° per unit of \(\sin\theta\) at 10.0 GHz, and the second line turns a measured phase back into an angle. A phase is known only within ±180°, so the angle is unambiguous while \(\sin\theta\) stays within \(\lambda/(2D) = 0.268\) of the steer direction’s \(\sin\theta_0\), which is ±15.5° at broadside and a little more in angle when the beam is steered.

    In the radar we take the two channels’ values in the range bin where CFAR declared the target. That bin holds the target’s echo with the 256-chirp integration gain of L27, and none of the clutter at other ranges, which would pull the angle toward itself. So the lab saves both subarray channels. Which side of broadside reads a positive angle depends on the order of the kit’s channels, so the lab checks it once, with a person on a mark to one side.

    A Walker on a Diagonal

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} R &= \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}, \qquad \theta = \arctan\frac{x}{y} \\ v_r &= -v\ \hat{\mathbf u}\cdot\hat{\mathbf r} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    • A diagonal walk changes the range and the angle together.

    • Its radial speed is the part of the walk along the line of sight.

    Put the array at the origin, \(y\) straight out along boresight and \(x\) to the side, positive on the E8 side. A point on the floor at \((x, y)\) sits at the range and angle of the first line. A walker moving at speed \(v\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf u}\) closes at the radial speed of the second line, where \(\hat{\mathbf r}\) is the unit vector from the array to the walker; the minus sign makes a closing walker positive, as on every page of this module.

    Work it for a walker who starts at \(P\), 0.4 m to the E8 side and 1.8 m out, and walks at 1.2 m/s toward \(Q\), 0.9 m to the E1 side and 3.4 m out:

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} R_P &= \sqrt{0.4^2 + 1.8^2} = 1.84\ \text{m}, \qquad \theta_P = \arctan\frac{0.4}{1.8} = +12.5^\circ, \\ \hat{\mathbf u} &= \frac{(-1.3,\ 1.6)}{2.06} = (-0.631,\ 0.776), \qquad \hat{\mathbf r}_P = \frac{(0.4,\ 1.8)}{1.84} = (0.217,\ 0.976), \\ v_{r,P} &= -1.2\left[(-0.631)(0.217) + (0.776)(0.976)\right] = -0.75\ \text{m/s}. \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    At \(P\) the walker opens at 0.75 m/s, less than its 1.2 m/s pace, because part of the walk runs across the line of sight. At \(Q\), 3.52 m out and −14.8°, the walk lines up more nearly with the line of sight and the walker opens at 1.09 m/s. A walker who turns and walks back has the same radial speeds with the sign reversed, and at the turn itself the speed, and with it the radial speed, passes through zero. That is the moment the 2-pulse canceller cuts the torso, and the moment the filter’s predict step carries the track.

    A walker moving exactly across the line of sight has \(v_r = v\cos 90^\circ = 0\) (L26), so the torso’s echo sits at 0 Hz and MTI removes it. A diagonal path keeps the Doppler from vanishing except at the turn.

    Why Monopulse Holds a Walker

    A walker crossing 4 m away at 1 m/s changes bearing by about 14° a second, so a beam sweep about a second long lets the walker leave the 13.6° beam between one look and the next. Monopulse measures once a frame, and in the example burst’s 102.4 ms the walker moves 1.5°, well inside the monopulse window, so the angle track keeps up.
