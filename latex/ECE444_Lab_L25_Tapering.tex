\textbf{The taper table, part 2: what the sweep reads.} Predict each row before
you press Start. The peak drop is measured against the frozen uniform trace, and
$\eta_t = (\sum a_n)^2 / (N \sum a_n^2)$ is computed rather than measured.

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{| l | K{0.6in} | K{0.65in} | K{0.6in} | K{0.95in} | K{0.9in} | K{0.45in} | K{0.55in} |}
\hline
\textbf{Preset} & \textbf{HPBW pred} & \textbf{HPBW meas} & \textbf{Drop pred} & \textbf{Drop meas} & \textbf{First sidelobe} & $\boldsymbol{\eta_t}$ & $\boldsymbol{\eta_t}$ \textbf{(dB)} \\\hline\hline
Uniform & \kv{$13.1\mydeg$} & \kv{$\approx 13\mydeg$} & \kv{$0$ dB} & \kv{$0$ dB} & \kv{$-11$ to $-13$ dBc} & \kv{1.00} & \kv{$0$} \\\hline
Hann & \kv{$19.5\mydeg$} & \kv{$19$ to $20\mydeg$} & \kv{$-4.7$ dB} & \kv{$-4.7 \pm 0.5$ dB} & \kv{below the noise floor} & \kv{0.75} & \kv{$-1.2$} \\\hline
Blackman & \kv{$23.1\mydeg$} & \kv{$22$ to $24\mydeg$} & \kv{$-6.1$ dB} & \kv{$-6.1 \pm 0.5$ dB} & \kv{below the noise floor} & \kv{0.66} & \kv{$-1.8$} \\\hline
Chebyshev & \kv{$24.3\mydeg$} & \kv{$23$ to $25\mydeg$} & \kv{$-6.5$ dB} & \kv{$-6.5 \pm 0.5$ dB} & \kv{below the noise floor} & \kv{0.62} & \kv{$-2.1$} \\\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{solution}
Work the Hann row through as the model. With
$a_n = 0.12,\ 0.43,\ 0.77,\ 1.00,\ 1.00,\ 0.77,\ 0.43,\ 0.12$,
\eq{
	\sum a_n &= 4.64, \qquad \sum a_n^2 = 3.584 \\
	\text{peak drop} &= 20\mylog{\frac{4.64}{8}} = 20\mylog{0.580} = -4.7 \text{ dB} \\
	\eta_t &= \frac{(4.64)^2}{8(3.584)} = \frac{21.53}{28.67} = 0.751 \quad \rightarrow \quad -1.2 \text{ dB}
}
The same two sums give $-6.1$ dB and $0.655$ for Blackman ($\sum a_n = 3.98$,
$\sum a_n^2 = 3.024$), and $-6.5$ dB and $0.621$ for Chebyshev
($\sum a_n = 3.78$, $\sum a_n^2 = 2.878$). Measured beamwidths land within a
degree or so of the prediction, because the sweep steps in $2.8125\mydeg$
increments and the 3 dB crossings fall between samples. Every tapered sidelobe
sits below the sweep's floor, which is about 23 dB under the uniform peak, so
the entry in that column is the phrase and not a number read off the grass.
\end{solution}

In one sentence, how did you arrive at these values?

\begin{solutionorlines}[1.2in]
Any symmetric taper that meets the specification is acceptable, so there is no
single set of eight numbers to look for. Grade the arithmetic instead: the peak
drop reported has to equal $20\mylog{\sum a_n / 8}$ for the gains written down.

percent in the middle, reads roughly $15\mydeg$ of beamwidth and a peak drop
near $-3.3$ dB, with the first sidelobe just reaching the floor. The last line
should describe two or three iterations: set the sliders, sweep, read the HPBW
and the sidelobe level, and adjust.
\end{solutionorlines}

\question \textbf{Written answers.} A short paragraph each.

\begin{parts}

	\part The tapered sidelobes disappear from the plot, but the beamwidth change
	is easy to see and easy to measure. Explain why the measurement gives a good
	number for one and no number at all for the other.

	\begin{solutionorlines}[2.2in]
	The beamwidth is read near the top of the trace, 3 dB below a peak that stands
	about 23 dB above the noise floor, so both crossings are well inside the
	dynamic range of the measurement. The tapered sidelobes are pushed more than
	17 dB below that peak, which puts them under the floor, and anything below the
	floor reads as noise rather than as pattern. The instrument is not measuring
	the sidelobe at all in those rows, so a number taken from the trace would
	report the receiver's noise. The correct entry is that the sidelobe is below
	the noise floor.
	\end{solutionorlines}

	\part Explain why the peak drop you measured is not the directivity the array
	lost, and what each number would be used for.

	\begin{solutionorlines}[2.6in]
	The peak drop is the coherent receive-voltage loss,
	$20\mylog{\sum a_n / N}$, read off a trace that is normalized to the uniform
	peak. The tapered array does collect less signal voltage from the source, and
	that is the $4.7$ dB the Hann trace falls. It also responds to less of
	everything arriving from off-boresight, and directivity is the ratio of
	on-axis intensity to the average over all angles, so that ratio falls only by
	the taper efficiency, $\eta_t = 0.75$, or $1.2$ dB. Re-normalizing each trace
	to its own peak would remove the $4.7$ dB from the plot entirely and leave the
	beam shape unchanged. Use the peak drop to predict what the plot will show and
	to check that the intended gains were loaded, and use $\eta_t$ in a link or
	system budget, where debiting $4.7$ dB would overstate the loss by a factor of
	two in power.
	\end{solutionorlines}

\end{parts}

\question \textbf{Grating lobes.} Source at boresight, Enforce Symmetric Taper
off. Lab preset 4 leaves Rx1, Rx4, and Rx7 on, so the active spacing is
$d_\text{eff} = 3d = 42$ mm. Then set Rx4 and Rx7 to 0 and Rx5 to 100 for
$d_\text{eff} = 4d = 56$ mm. A full-height lobe appears at every angle where
$\sin\theta = m\lambda/d_\text{eff}$ has a real solution. Predict the angles
before each sweep. The peak drop is read against the frozen uniform trace, and
its calculated value follows from the number of elements still on.

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{| l | K{0.5in} | K{0.9in} | K{0.95in} | K{0.8in} | K{0.75in} | K{0.75in} |}
\hline
\textbf{Elements on} & $\boldsymbol{d_\text{eff}}$ & \textbf{Lobes calc} & \textbf{Lobes meas} & \textbf{Height vs $0\mydeg$ lobe} & \textbf{Drop calc} & \textbf{Drop meas} \\\hline\hline
All eight & 14 mm & \kv{none} & \kv{none} & \kv{--} & \kv{$0$ dB} & \kv{$0$ dB} \\\hline
Rx1, Rx4, Rx7 & 42 mm & \kv{$\pm 43\mydeg$} & \kv{$\pm 42$ to $\pm 43\mydeg$} & \kv{within 0.5 dB} & \kv{$-8.5$ dB} & \kv{$-8$ to $-9$ dB} \\\hline
Rx1, Rx5 & 56 mm & \kv{$\pm 31\mydeg$ (and $\pm 90\mydeg$)} & \kv{$\pm 30$ to $\pm 33\mydeg$, both ends} & \kv{within 0.5 dB} & \kv{$-12.0$ dB} & \kv{$-11$ to $-12$ dB} \\\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{solution}
At 10.525 GHz, $\lambda = 28.5$ mm. With all eight elements on,
$\lambda/d = 2.03$, so $\sin\theta = \pm 2.03$ has no real solution and the
only lobe is the main one. At 42 mm, $\lambda/d_\text{eff} = 0.678$ and
$\theta = \sin^{-1}(0.678) = 42.7\mydeg$; the $m = 2$ solution would need
$\sin\theta = 1.36$. At 56 mm, $\lambda/d_\text{eff} = 0.509$ gives
$30.6\mydeg$, and $m = 2$ needs $\sin\theta = 1.02$, just past the horizon,
so the trace rises to full height at the $\pm 90\mydeg$ ends without quite
showing a peak. (At the workshop's 10.3 GHz the same arithmetic gives
$44\mydeg$ and $31\mydeg$, which is why Lesson 26 quotes $\pm 44\mydeg$.)
The peak drops are the coherent sums again: $20\mylog(3/8) = -8.5$ dB and
$20\mylog(2/8) = -12.0$ dB. The sweep reads a decibel or so above those,
because the noise adds to a weaker peak.
\end{solution}

In one sentence: every grating lobe stands as tall as the main lobe, and no
sidelobe ever does. What is the difference between the two?

\begin{solutionorlines}[1.0in]
A grating lobe is a second direction in which every element's contribution
adds in phase, so the array factor there is exactly its maximum, while a
sidelobe is a partial cancellation that never reaches it.
\end{solutionorlines}

\question \textbf{Beam squint.} Move the HB100 to about $45\mydeg$ on the
protractor arc and press the Uniform preset. Lab preset 5 loads Signal BW =
500 MHz: the GUI computes the steering phases for a frequency 500 MHz below
the Signal Freq and measures at the Signal Freq, which is what a signal at
the edge of a 500 MHz band sees. Set Signal BW to 10 MHz, sweep, read Est.
Angle, and press Freeze. Then set it back to 500 MHz and sweep again. The
calculated shift comes from the squint relation in Lesson 26: a beam
commanded to $\theta_0$ with its phases set at $f_0$ points at
$\sin^{-1}\!\left[(f_0/f)\sin\theta_0\right]$ when observed at $f$.

\begin{center}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{| l | K{1.1in} | K{1.1in} | K{1.1in} |}
\hline
\textbf{Signal BW} & \textbf{Est. Angle meas} & \textbf{Shift calc} & \textbf{Shift meas} \\\hline\hline
10 MHz & \kv{$\approx 45\mydeg$ (the source)} & \kv{$0.1\mydeg$} & \kv{--} \\\hline
500 MHz & \kv{$\approx 48\mydeg$} & \kv{$+2.9\mydeg$} & \kv{$+2.5$ to $+3.5\mydeg$} \\\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{solution}
With $f = 10.525$ GHz and $f_0 = 10.025$ GHz, a beam commanded to
$45\mydeg$ points at $\sin^{-1}(0.9525 \times 0.707) = 42.3\mydeg$: the same
phases are a shorter electrical delay at the higher frequency, so the beam
leans back toward broadside. The sweep therefore finds the source only when it
commands the angle whose squinted beam lands on $45\mydeg$,
$\sin\theta_c = (f/f_0)\sin 45\mydeg = 0.742$, or $\theta_c = 47.9\mydeg$.
The Est. Angle readout moves $+2.9\mydeg$ while the beam itself moved
$-2.7\mydeg$; the two differ slightly because the squint is not symmetric in
angle. At 10 MHz the same arithmetic gives $0.06\mydeg$, below the sweep grid.
\end{solution}

The source did not move between the two sweeps. In one sentence, what did?

\begin{solutionorlines}[1.0in]
The phase ramp was computed for 10.025 GHz, and at 10.525 GHz that ramp is a
smaller fraction of a period per element, so every commanded beam points
closer to broadside than its label says and the sweep has to command a
larger angle to reach the source.
\end{solutionorlines}

\question \textbf{Phase quantization.} Source back at boresight. Lab preset 6
loads the Blackman taper, so every true sidelobe is below the floor and
anything that appears is the phase shifter's doing. Turn \textbf{Use Bits}
off, so the sweep keeps stepping the commanded angle by $2.8125\mydeg$ while
the Phase Shift Bits slider coarsens the element phases. Sweep at 7, 4, 3, and
2 bits. The Blackman main lobe is about $24\mydeg$ wide and its skirt reaches
the floor near $\pm 35\mydeg$, so record the highest lobe \emph{beyond}
$\pm 35\mydeg$ relative to the peak, and beside it the Lesson 26 rule of thumb
for the quantization sidelobe level, $-6B$ dB.

\begin{center}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{| c | K{0.9in} | K{1.5in} | K{1.1in} |}
\hline
\textbf{Bits} $\boldsymbol{B}$ & \textbf{LSB calc} & \textbf{Highest lobe beyond $\pm 35\mydeg$, meas} & $\boldsymbol{-6B}$ \textbf{dB} \\\hline\hline
7 & \kv{$2.8125\mydeg$} & \kv{below the noise floor} & \kv{$-42$ dB} \\\hline
4 & \kv{$22.5\mydeg$} & \kv{$-15$ to $-21$ dBc, just above the floor} & \kv{$-24$ dB} \\\hline
3 & \kv{$45\mydeg$} & \kv{$-12$ to $-15$ dBc} & \kv{$-18$ dB} \\\hline
2 & \kv{$90\mydeg$} & \kv{$-8$ to $-10$ dBc} & \kv{$-12$ dB} \\\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{solution}
The LSB is $360\mydeg/2^B$. At 7 bits the quantized ramp is within
$1.4\mydeg$ of the ideal one everywhere, and the Blackman sidelobes stay under
the floor, which is consistent with a $-42$ dB estimate. From 4 bits down the
lobes come up out of the floor a few decibels at a time and sit 3 to 6 dB
above the rule of thumb. The rule is a root-mean-square level for a large
array in which the rounding errors scatter like noise; eight elements with a
ramp rounded to $45\mydeg$ or $90\mydeg$ form a staircase whose error is
periodic across the aperture, and a periodic error makes a few distinct lobes
rather than a floor, so the highest of them stands above the RMS figure.
Where the lobes land depends on the commanded angle: the sweep passes through
every one, which is why it finds the worst cases.
\end{solution}

The rule of thumb puts the 7-bit lobes near $-42$ dB. In one sentence, why
does that row have no number, and what is the deepest level this sweep could
report for this taper?

\begin{solutionorlines}[1.1in]
The sweep's floor sits about 23 dB under the uniform peak, and the Blackman
peak is itself 6 dB lower, so the floor is only 17 to 18 dB under this trace's
peak; anything the rule predicts below that is not measurable here, and a
number read from the grass would be the receiver's noise.
\end{solutionorlines}

Before you leave: Use Bits on, Phase Shift Bits 7, Signal BW 10 MHz, and the
Uniform preset.
