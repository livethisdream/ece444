\question \textbf{The bearing error budget.} Est. Angle is the steer angle of the
largest sample in the beam sweep, and the sweep steps the progressive phase one
ADAR1000 LSB, $360\mydeg/2^7 = 2.8125\mydeg$, at a time.

\begin{parts}

	\part Find $kd$ in degrees, the sweep's step in $\sin\theta$, and the step in
	angle at each Est. Angle in the table. Then give the largest error the grid
	alone can cause at each angle.

	\begin{center}
	\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
	\begin{tabular}{| l | K{1.1in} | K{1.1in} | K{1.1in} |}
	\hline
	Est. Angle & $0\mydeg$ & $30\mydeg$ & $45\mydeg$ \\\hline
	Grid step & \kv{$0.91\mydeg$} & \kv{$1.05\mydeg$} & \kv{$1.29\mydeg$} \\\hline
	Largest grid error & \kv{$0.46\mydeg$} & \kv{$0.53\mydeg$} & \kv{$0.64\mydeg$} \\\hline
	\end{tabular}
	\end{center}

		\begin{solution}[1.2in]
		\eq{ \lambda &= \frac{3.00\times 10^{8}}{10.525\times 10^{9}} = 28.50\ \text{mm}, \qquad
		     kd = 360\mydeg\ \frac{14}{28.50} = 176.8\mydeg \\
		     \delta(\sin\theta) &= \frac{2.8125\mydeg}{176.8\mydeg} = 0.0159, \qquad
		     \delta\theta = \frac{0.0159}{\cosp{\theta}}\ \text{rad} }
		The step widens as $1/\cos\theta$, and Est. Angle can miss the source by
		half a step.
		\end{solution}

	\part You will aim each kit's broadside at the circle's center mark by eye,
	which holds the heading to about $1.5\mydeg$. Take $1.0\mydeg$ for multipath
	and $0.2\mydeg$ for noise. Combine these with the grid term at $30\mydeg$ to
	find the bearing uncertainty $\sigma_\beta$. Then repeat with the heading set
	carefully to $1.0\mydeg$.

	\begin{center}
	$\sigma_\beta$ (heading $1.5\mydeg$) = \ansbox[1.1in]{$1.9\mydeg$}
	\hspace{0.4in}
	$\sigma_\beta$ (heading $1.0\mydeg$) = \ansbox[1.1in]{$1.5\mydeg$}
	\end{center}

		\begin{solution}[1.0in]
		The terms are independent, so they add as a root sum of squares:
		\eq{ \sigma_\beta &= \sqrt{0.53^2 + 1.5^2 + 1.0^2 + 0.2^2} = 1.9\mydeg \\
		     &= \sqrt{0.53^2 + 1.0^2 + 1.0^2 + 0.2^2} = 1.5\mydeg }
		The heading is the largest term in both, so it is where care pays off.
		\end{solution}

	\part A teammate sets a kit on the floor, $0.75\ \m$ below the boxes, and
	$3\ \m$ from a box that is $30\mydeg$ off broadside in azimuth. What does Est.
	Angle read, and which way does the error always pull?

	\begin{solution}[1.2in]
	The box is $\tan^{-1}(0.75/3) = 14.0\mydeg$ above the array's plane. A line
	array measures the cone angle:
	\eq{ \sin\theta_\text{meas} = \sinp{30\mydeg}\cosp{14.0\mydeg} = 0.485, \qquad \theta_\text{meas} = 29.0\mydeg }
	The error always pulls the reading toward broadside, since $\cos(\text{el}) \le 1$.
	Keep the kits on tables at the boxes' height.
	\end{solution}

\end{parts}

\question \textbf{Where to stand.} The keep-out circle has a radius of
$1.5\ \m$, and the boxes face the room's $-y$ direction, so every kit position
must lie within $\pm 60\mydeg$ of the $-y$ axis as seen from the center mark.
Your room's radius may differ; the instructor gives the actual value at the
start of the period. Take $\sigma_\beta = 2\mydeg$ in this question.

\begin{parts}

	\part Find the cross-range error of one bearing line at $3$, $4$, and
	$5\ \m$ from the source.

	\begin{center}
	\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
	\begin{tabular}{| l | K{1.1in} | K{1.1in} | K{1.1in} |}
	\hline
	Range & $3\ \m$ & $4\ \m$ & $5\ \m$ \\\hline
	Cross-range error & \kv{$0.10\ \m$} & \kv{$0.14\ \m$} & \kv{$0.17\ \m$} \\\hline
	\end{tabular}
	\end{center}

		\begin{solution}[0.5in]
		$\delta_\perp = R\tanp{\sigma_\beta}$; at $4\ \m$, $4\tanp{2\mydeg} = 0.14\ \m$.
		\end{solution}

	\part Two kits $4\ \m$ from the source see it along bearings that cross at
	angle $\gamma$. Find the RMS error of the two-bearing fix at each crossing
	angle.

	\begin{center}
	\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
	\begin{tabular}{| l | K{1.1in} | K{1.1in} | K{1.1in} |}
	\hline
	$\gamma$ & $90\mydeg$ & $30\mydeg$ & $15\mydeg$ \\\hline
	RMS fix error & \kv{$0.20\ \m$} & \kv{$0.40\ \m$} & \kv{$0.76\ \m$} \\\hline
	\end{tabular}
	\end{center}

		\begin{solution}[0.6in]
		$\sigma_\text{fix} = \sqrt{2}\ \delta_\perp/\sin\gamma$ with
		$\delta_\perp = 0.14\ \m$: $0.20$, $0.40$, and $0.76\ \m$.
		\end{solution}

	\part Every box must stay within $\pm 30\mydeg$ of each kit's broadside. A kit
	stands a distance $D$ from the center mark with its broadside aimed at the
	center. Find the smallest $D$ for which the whole circle stays within
	$\pm 30\mydeg$ of broadside.

	\begin{center}
	$D_\text{min}$ = \ansbox[1.1in]{$3.0\ \m$}
	\end{center}

		\begin{solution}[1.0in]
		The circle's edge is seen at its widest where the line of sight is
		tangent to it, at an angle $\sin^{-1}(a/D)$ from the line to the center.
		Setting that to $30\mydeg$ gives
		\eq{ D_\text{min} = \frac{a}{\sinp{30\mydeg}} = 2a = 3.0\ \m }
		\end{solution}

	\part Sketch the circle and mark where your team plans to put its two kits
	and the second position each kit will move to, so that the team takes four
	bearings. Choose positions inside the $\pm 60\mydeg$ arc that keep every box
	within $\pm 30\mydeg$ of broadside and give steep crossings for a box anywhere
	in the circle. Label each
	position with its distance from the center and its direction in room angles.

	\begin{solution}[2.4in]
	One good plan uses the full arc: kit A at $(-3.0, -1.8)$ and $(-1.2, -3.3)\ \m$,
	kit B at $(1.2, -3.3)$ and $(3.0, -1.8)\ \m$, each $3.5\ \m$ out and aimed at
	the center. The positions sit at $211\mydeg$, $250\mydeg$, $290\mydeg$, and
	$329\mydeg$ from the center, so the outer pair crosses at about $120\mydeg$ and
	neighbors at about $40\mydeg$, and the widest box sits $25\mydeg$ off
	broadside. Any plan with the kits at least $2a$ out and spread across most of
	the arc meets the brief.
	\end{solution}

	\part The five boxes are at least $0.75\ \m$ apart, and your team names the
	box nearest its fix. A fix closer to a wrong box than to the right one has to
	miss by at least half the spacing. Express half the spacing in units of the
	per-axis error, $0.14\ \m$, of a $90\mydeg$ crossing, and say in one sentence
	whether naming the wrong box should be common.

	\begin{solution}[0.9in]
	$0.375/0.14 = 2.7$ standard deviations. A miss that large along the line to
	the nearest neighbor happens well under 1\% of the time, so a team that keeps
	its crossing steep and its budget near $2\mydeg$ should name the right box;
	a simulation of a row of boxes gives 99\% at this spacing.
	\end{solution}

\end{parts}

\question \textbf{The triangulation script.} Write a Python function that takes
the positions $\mathbf p_i$ of the kits and their room bearings $\beta_i$ and
returns the least-squares fix and each bearing's residual. Lesson 19's "Many
Bearings: Least Squares" gives the normal equations. Use numpy; do not use
MATLAB. Test it on this practice set from an earlier class, in which each kit
stood inside the arc, aimed its broadside at the center mark, and read positive
Est. Angle counterclockwise:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{| c | c | c | c | K{0.9in} |}
\hline
\textbf{Bearing} & \textbf{Position (m)} & \textbf{Heading $h$} & \textbf{Est. Angle} & \textbf{Room bearing $\beta$} \\\hline\hline
1 & $(-3.0,\ -1.8)$ & $31.0\mydeg$  & $+1.4\mydeg$  & \kv{$32.4\mydeg$} \\\hline
2 & $(-1.2,\ -3.3)$ & $70.0\mydeg$  & $-6.7\mydeg$  & \kv{$63.3\mydeg$} \\\hline
3 & $(1.2,\ -3.3)$  & $110.0\mydeg$ & $-12.0\mydeg$ & \kv{$98.0\mydeg$} \\\hline
4 & $(3.0,\ -1.8)$  & $149.0\mydeg$ & $-3.5\mydeg$  & \kv{$145.5\mydeg$} \\\hline
\end{tabular}

\vspace{6pt}
Boxes: \textbf{1} $(-0.9,\ 0.6)$, \textbf{2} $(-0.2,\ -0.8)$,
\textbf{3} $(0.6,\ 0.4)$, \textbf{4} $(0.0,\ 1.1)$, \textbf{5} $(0.9,\ -0.5)$ m
\end{center}

\begin{parts}

	\part Fill in the room bearings, run your function on all four, and record
	the fix and the four residuals. Attach your code.

	\begin{center}
	\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
	\begin{tabular}{| l | K{1.0in} | K{0.75in} | K{0.75in} | K{0.75in} | K{0.75in} |}
	\hline
	 & \textbf{Fix (m)} & $e_1$ & $e_2$ & $e_3$ & $e_4$ \\\hline
	All four & \kv{$(0.51,\ 0.18)$} & \kv{$-0.21$} & \kv{$0.04$} & \kv{$0.20$} & \kv{$-0.22$} \\\hline
	\end{tabular}
	\end{center}

		\begin{solution}
		$\beta = h + \theta_\text{est}$. One version of the function:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import numpy as np

def fix(p, beta_deg):
    """Least-squares crossing of bearing lines.
    p: (K, 2) kit positions in m; beta_deg: (K,) room bearings in deg."""
    b = np.radians(beta_deg)
    n = np.column_stack([-np.sin(b), np.cos(b)])  # unit normal to each line
    c = np.sum(n * p, axis=1)                      # n . p for each line
    x = np.linalg.solve(n.T @ n, n.T @ c)          # normal equations
    return x, n @ x - c                            # fix, signed residuals

p = np.array([[-3.0, -1.8], [-1.2, -3.3], [1.2, -3.3], [3.0, -1.8]])
beta = np.array([31.0, 70.0, 110.0, 149.0]) + np.array([1.4, -6.7, -12.0, -3.5])
x, e = fix(p, beta)
\end{lstlisting}
		The signs of the residuals depend on the normal's direction; their sizes
		are what matter.
		\end{solution}

	leave a residual near $4\tanp{2\mydeg} = 0.14\ \m$. Which bearing is the
	outlier? Drop it, solve again with the other three, and record the new fix and
	residuals.

	\begin{center}
	\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
	\begin{tabular}{| l | K{1.0in} | K{0.75in} | K{0.75in} | K{0.75in} |}
	\hline
	 & \textbf{Fix (m)} & \multicolumn{3}{c|}{\textbf{Residuals of the three kept}} \\\hline
	Outlier dropped & \kv{$(0.68,\ 0.51)$} & \kv{$-0.02$} & \kv{$0.03$} & \kv{$-0.02$} \\\hline
	\end{tabular}
	\end{center}

		\begin{solution}[1.3in]
		Bearing 4 has the largest residual, $0.22\ \m$, twice its
		$0.11\ \m$ cross-range error; bearings 1 and 3 are also large because
		least squares spreads one bad line's error over the others. Dropping
		bearing 4 leaves residuals of $0.02$ to $0.03\ \m$, well inside the budget,
		which confirms it. Bearing 4 read $8\mydeg$ high, which is the size of
		error a reflection off a nearby surface produces.
		\end{solution}

	\part Name the box from each fix, and give each fix's distance to that box.

	\begin{solution}[0.9in]

	$0.79\ \m$ from the first fix and $0.90\ \m$ from the second. The outlier did
	not change the answer here, but it nearly doubled the miss.
	\end{solution}

\question \textbf{Reconcile the fix.} The instructor posts the live box's measured
position after the period. Answer each part in three or four sentences, using
your numbers from Part 2 and your predictions from Part 1.

\begin{parts}

	\part Give the distance from your fix to the live box, and compare it with the
	RMS fix error you predicted for your geometry in Question 2. Is the miss
	consistent with your budget?
	\begin{solution}[1.5in]
	With kits $3.5$ to $4\ \m$ out and a crossing near $90\mydeg$, the predicted
	RMS error is about $0.2\ \m$ at $\sigma_\beta = 2\mydeg$. A miss up to about
	twice that is consistent. A larger miss points at one bad bearing or at a
	systematic error common to a kit: a wrong $s$, a heading off by several
	degrees, or a kit below the boxes.
	\end{solution}

	\part Use the live box's position to find the true bearing from each of your
	four positions, and the error of each measured bearing. Which term of your
	error budget best explains the largest one?
	\begin{solution}[1.8in]
	Each true bearing is $\text{atan2}(y_\text{box} - y_i,\ x_\text{box} - x_i)$.
	Errors of $1$ to $3\mydeg$ are within the budget, and the heading is the
	usual dominant term. An error that is the same sign and size at both of one
	kit's positions points at that kit's heading or its $s$; one large error at a
	single position points at a reflection, especially if that position's Peak
	Array Gain was low or the spread was large.
	\end{solution}

	\part If your residuals flagged an outlier, say whether dropping it moved the
	fix toward the live box or away from it, and what in the room you think caused
	it. If no residual stood out, say what that tells you about the four
	bearings.
	\begin{solution}[1.5in]
	A real outlier is confirmed when dropping it shrinks the other residuals and
	moves the fix toward the box. Whiteboards, metal cabinets, and people standing
	near a line of sight are the usual causes. If every residual stayed near
	$R_i\tanp{\sigma_\beta}$, the four bearings agree within the budget and the
	least-squares fix is the best estimate the data allow.
	\end{solution}

\end{parts}

	\part Record the three two-bearing fixes and the crossing angle of each pair.
	Which pair landed closest to the live box, and is that the pair with the
	steepest crossing?
	\begin{solution}[2.0in]
	The crossing angle of a pair is the difference of its two room bearings. The
	pair with the crossing nearest $90\mydeg$ usually lands closest, since its
	error is $\sqrt{2}\ \delta_\perp/\sin\gamma$. A shallow pair that happens to
	land close has errors that happened to cancel along its long axis; the
	residual-free two-bearing fix cannot tell you which case you are in.
	\end{solution}

	\part In two or three sentences, explain why a two-bearing fix can never
	reveal its own bad bearing, and why your team took four.
	\begin{solution}[1.3in]
	Two lines always cross at exactly one point, so every two-bearing fix has
	zero residuals whether the bearings are good or bad. Only a third line can
	disagree with the first two. Four bearings let the team drop one and still
	keep a check on the remaining three.
	\end{solution}
