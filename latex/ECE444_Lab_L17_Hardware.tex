\question \textbf{The identity reset.} Every team flashed its card from the
same golden image, and on its first boot each card regenerates its SSH host
keys and its \texttt{/etc/machine-id} before the ssh server starts. Explain in
three or four sentences why a cloned card must do this.

\begin{solutionorlines}[1.8in]
A card flashed from the golden image is a byte-for-byte copy of the golden kit,
so without the reset every kit would present the same SSH host keys and the
same machine-id. The host key is how ssh identifies the machine it reached;
with identical keys a laptop's \texttt{known\_hosts} cannot tell the kits
apart, and a key copied off one kit would impersonate all of them. The
machine-id is what systemd-networkd derives its DHCP client identity from, so
kits sharing one request the same lease on a shared network and take turns
losing it, which looks like a Pi dropping off the network at random.
Regenerating both before ssh starts gives each kit its own identity, and the
reboot that follows makes the new machine-id take effect, since the init system
reads it only at boot.
\end{solutionorlines}

\question \textbf{The frequency plan.} Copy the frequency \textbf{Find HB100}
reported in Part A, then work the high-side mixing arithmetic that follows from
it. The IF is fixed by the filtering after the
mixer, so the LO is the only frequency that moves when the source moves.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{| l | K{2.4in} |}
\hline
\textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{Value} \\\hline\hline
Measured HB100 frequency $f_{\text{RF}}$ & \kv{your own, in $10.1$--$10.7\ \ghz$} \\\hline
Required LO frequency $f_{\text{LO}}$ & \kv{$f_{\text{RF}} + 2.2\ \ghz$} \\\hline
Resulting IF $f_{\text{IF}}$ & \kv{$2.200\ \ghz$} \\\hline
Is the LO inside $12.2$--$13.0\ \ghz$? & \kv{yes, for any HB100 in that band} \\\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Show the arithmetic for your measured frequency here.

\begin{solutionorlines}[1.1in]
For a nominal source at $10.525\ \ghz$, high-side injection puts the LO at
\eq{
	f_{\text{LO}} = f_{\text{RF}} + f_{\text{IF}} = 10.525 + 2.200 = 12.725\ \ghz
}
That LO sits inside the $12.2$ to $13.0\ \ghz$ VCO range, so the setting is
reachable, and the mixer difference, $12.725 - 10.525\ \ghz$, lands on the
$2.2\ \ghz$ IF the Pluto is tuned to. Any measured frequency in $10.1$ to
$10.7\ \ghz$ gives an LO inside the range, which is why the hardware covers every
HB100 unit the lab issues.
\end{solutionorlines}

\question \textbf{The Labeled Block Diagram.} Sketch the receive chain from the
patches to the Raspberry Pi. Label every block with its name and with the
frequency present at that point, mark where the analog summing happens, and mark
where the two digital channels begin.

\begin{solutionorbox}[3.4in]
Patches ($f_{\text{RF}}$, eight of them, $d = 14$ mm apart) $\rightarrow$ ADL8107
LNA behind each patch ($f_{\text{RF}}$) $\rightarrow$ two ADAR1000 beamformers,
four elements each, phase and gain per element, \textbf{analog summing happens
here} ($f_{\text{RF}}$, two outputs) $\rightarrow$ two LTC5548 mixers driven by
the ADF4159 PLL and HMC735 VCO at $f_{\text{LO}} = f_{\text{RF}} + 2.2\ \ghz$
($12.2$ to $13.0\ \ghz$) $\rightarrow$ two IF channels at $2.2\ \ghz$
$\rightarrow$ ADALM-Pluto (AD9361), tuned to $2.2\ \ghz$, sampling at 3 MSPS,
\textbf{the digital channels begin here} $\rightarrow$ Raspberry Pi over USB,
running the backend and serving the browser interface.
\end{solutionorbox}

\question \textbf{FFT observations.} Work on the FFT tab with the source at
$1\ \m$ and record what the spectrum does as you change two controls.

\begin{parts}

	\part Where does the tone sit on the FFT tab's frequency axis, and how far
	above the noise floor is it?

	\vspace{6pt}
	\begin{center}
	Peak offset = \ansbox[1.7in]{near $0\ \mhz$} %
	Peak above floor = \ansbox[1.7in]{$\ge 20\db$}
	\end{center}
	\vspace{6pt}

	\begin{solution}
	Find HB100 sets the LO to the measured frequency plus 2.2 GHz, so on hardware
	the tone sits near 0 MHz, within about $\pm 1\ \mhz$. The simulator fixes its
	tone 1 MHz from center, and the GUI's flipped axis shows it at $-1\ \mhz$.
	\end{solution}

	\part Set \textbf{Rx Gain (dB)} to 10 and record the peak and floor, then
	repeat at 0 and at 20. Report the peak and floor levels in dBFS (decibels
	relative to the ADC's full-scale input) and the separation between them in dB.

	\vspace{4pt}
	\begin{center}
	\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
	\begin{tabular}{| l | K{1.25in} | K{1.25in} | K{1.35in} |}
	\hline
	\textbf{Rx Gain} & \textbf{Peak (dBFS)} & \textbf{Floor (dBFS)} & \textbf{Separation (dB)} \\\hline\hline
	0 dB & \kv{about $10$ dB below the 10 dB row} & \kv{about $10$ dB below the 10 dB row} & \kv{same, or a few dB less} \\\hline
	10 dB & \kv{reference} & \kv{reference} & \kv{$\ge 20$ dB} \\\hline
	20 dB & \kv{up to $10$ dB above; less if compressed} & \kv{about $10$ dB above the 10 dB row} & \kv{same, or less if the peak compresses} \\\hline
	\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	\vspace{4pt}

	\begin{solution}
	Both levels move together with the slider, because the SDR applies this gain
	internally, long after the LNAs have already set the noise level that
	accompanies the signal. The separation therefore holds roughly constant across
	the three settings. It can shrink at 0 dB, where the SDR's own noise starts to
	contribute, and at 20 dB the peak may rise by less than 10 dB because the
	converter is near full scale and compressing. A separation that changes by much
	more than a few dB across the three rows points to a saturating or misaimed setup.
	\end{solution}

	\part Lower \textbf{Signal Freq (GHz)} by $0.001\ \ghz$ (one click of the
	field's down arrow) and press Enter, then record how far the peak moves and in
	which direction. (On an older kit, press \textbf{Stop} and then \textbf{Start}
	to send the new value.)

	\vspace{6pt}
	\begin{center}
	Peak shift = \ansbox[2.0in]{$+1\ \mhz$ (up the axis)}
	\end{center}
	\vspace{6pt}

	\begin{solutionorlines}[0.9in]
	The GUI holds the IF fixed and moves the LO, so lowering the assumed source
	frequency by 1 MHz lowers the LO by 1 MHz. The source has not moved, so its IF
	drops by 1 MHz, and the GUI's flipped axis plots the peak 1 MHz higher: the
	source now sits 1 MHz above Signal Freq. The window is only 3 MHz wide at
	3 MSPS, so an error larger than about 1.5 MHz moves the tone off the display,
	which is what a bad \textbf{Find HB100} result looks like.
	\end{solutionorlines}

\end{parts}

\question \textbf{Written answers.} Answer each in three or four sentences.

\begin{parts}

	\part Why does the control software have to measure the HB100's frequency
	instead of assuming $10.525\ \ghz$?

	\begin{solutionorlines}[1.7in]
	The HB100's oscillator is a free-running dielectric resonator, not a locked
	synthesizer, so its output frequency is set by the mechanical dimensions of
	that resonator and drifts with temperature. Units land anywhere from about
	$10.1$ to $10.7\ \ghz$. Every LO setting and every steering phase the backend
	computes depends on the actual frequency: an LO set for the wrong frequency
	puts the tone outside the 3 MHz window, and the steering phase
	$\Delta\phi = kd\sin\theta_0$ scales with frequency through $k = 2\pi f/c$. So
	the software sweeps the LO, watches where the IF tone appears, and records the
	answer instead of assuming one.
	\end{solutionorlines}

	\part The array has eight elements, but software sees only two digital
	channels. Explain where the other six went, and name one measurement this
	rules out.

	\begin{solutionorlines}[1.9in]
	Each ADAR1000 applies a phase and a gain to its four elements at RF and then
	sums those four into a single output, so the individual element signals stop
	existing before anything is digitized. Two subarray sums reach the Pluto, and
	software cannot recover the eight per-element signals from them. That rules
	out any measurement that needs per-element data after the fact, such as forming
	several independent beams from one set of samples (two channels can form more
	than one beam, but each is a combination of the same two subarray patterns), or
	running an adaptive beamformer with more than one null. The MVDR run in
	Lesson 28 has exactly two digital degrees of freedom for this reason, and one of
	them holds the look direction.
	\end{solutionorlines}

\end{parts}

\end{questions}
